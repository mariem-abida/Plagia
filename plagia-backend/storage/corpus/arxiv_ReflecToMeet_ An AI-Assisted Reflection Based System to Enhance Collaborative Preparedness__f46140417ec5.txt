In collaborative settings, difficulties in sustaining a consistent pace and engagement often lead to task drift, reducing preparedness and overall
effectiveness between meetings. To address this, we conducted a formative study and developed ReflecToMeet , an AI-assisted system that
integrates theory-driven reflective prompts with mechanisms for sharing teammates’ reflections. Informed by ten formative interviews,
the system was evaluated in a mixed-method study across three conditions: (1) deeper reflection, (2) regular reflection, and (3) control
(unstructured reflection). Participants in the control condition demonstrated less deliberate thought and weaker collaboration, which led
to stress and misalignment during team meetings. In contrast, structured reflection supported greater organization and steadier progress.
The deeper reflection condition further facilitated confidence, teamwork, and idea generation, although it imposed a higher cognitive load.
We also discuss design implications for AI agents that facilitate reflection to enhance collaboration and outline broader considerations for
AI-assisted systems aimed at sustaining collaborative goals.

Introduction
Modern collaborative work is closely tied to online meetings, which have become a standard practice across professional,
academic, and social domains since the COVID-19 pandemic [42]. These meetings require sustained attention to enable
planning, note-taking, and decision-making. Yet participants frequently miss important details, either due to multitasking [14]
or environmental distractions that disrupt focus [56]. To address these challenges, prior research has introduced strategies
such as real-time cues to maintain engagement [2, 16], interventions when discussions drift off course [17], mechanisms to
ensure balanced participation [35], tools to counteract groupthink by deepening the level of dialogue [58], and live feedback
on team dynamics to promote more inclusive collaboration [84]. Preparedness before meetings has also been studied, with an
emphasis on strengthening intentionality before participants convene. Research has shown that articulating mental models of
meeting goals improves alignment [91], and subsequent work has demonstrated how LLM-driven chat-based premortems
support participants in clarifying success criteria and anticipating challenges through reflection [90].
Post-meeting approaches in the meeting lifecycle emphasize reflection on behaviors, decisions, and coordination to
strengthen follow-up work for subsequent sessions. MeetingCoach provides a dashboard summarizing turn-taking, sentiment,
and action items to enhance inclusiveness [83], and Retrospector facilitates collaborative reflection after meetings to improve
team practices [80]. Other studies focus on preserving key details after meetings through digital artifacts [111] and detailed
summaries [3, 30], enabling participants to review and reflect asynchronously. Asynchronous activity following synchronous
communication has become a growing area of interest since the pandemic [40, 115], as remote collaboration often unfolds
through a series of meetings interleaved with individual and asynchronous work. While drifting off-track during a meeting is
not uncommon [96], sustaining continuity and momentum in the longer gaps between meetings is even more difficult, as
attentional states naturally shift over time and make individuals more susceptible to distraction and drift [66].
Within the HCI and CHI community, prior work has proposed many ways to improve meeting effectiveness. One thread of
research focuses on reflection around meetings as a way to improve collaboration through reflection-in-action. This includes
AI-driven support before meetings [90, 91], during meetings [35, 56, 58], and after meetings [83, 111]. Yet the interval between
meetings often receives little attention, even though this is the period when participants must sustain engagement and
motivation independently. In settings where collaboration takes place without formal hierarchy and the goal is not immediate,
derailment and task drift frequently occur [65, 113, 121]. In the absence of an immediate objective, individuals often experience
a decline in motivation and defer meaningful progress until the next external checkpoint, such as a scheduled meeting or
impending deadline. The decline in motivation after meetings aligns with previous research on deadline-driven behavior.
According to Temporal Motivation Theory [98], motivation tends to rise as deadlines draw near and decline when goals seem
far away. Therefore, when the next checkpoint appears, participants may exert only minimal effort aimed at maintaining the
appearance of engagement [8, 36, 89, 97].
To mitigate derailment during long intervals between meetings, it is important for participants to remain regularly engaged
with their tasks. Personal tracking tools or journals can provide some support, but are designed for individual use and rarely
support shared accountability or align with group goals. Prior work has shown that effective support in group settings
requires linking personal reflection with collective awareness [80, 111]. Without this connection, individual efforts risk
drifting away from the team’s objectives. To investigate how momentum is maintained between meetings, we conducted a
formative study in which we interviewed students about their practices of task management, distribution, and preparation
around meetings. The study identified key design considerations that help maintain engagement between meetings and
support collaborative progress. Building on these findings, we designed ReflecToMeet , an AI-assisted system that addresses
two core needs: (1) providing personalized reflective prompts and summaries to support collaborative preparedness, and
(2) sharing team members’ progress in the form of reflections to keep others informed and focused. By guiding users with
reflective questions and making their partners’ thought processes visible, ReflecToMeet helps participants remain engaged and
better prepared for collaboration. ReflecToMeet is neither a personal tracking tool nor designed to replace one; its focus is on
sustaining engagement and preparedness in collaborative settings. We aim to encourage users to pause and reflect deeply,
helping them engage more meaningfully with the task at hand. We hypothesize that this will help them be better prepared
by the time the next checkpoint or meeting arrives, increasing their confidence, improving their performance, and enabling
them to contribute more effectively to the collaboration. Therefore, we designed ReflecToMeet around Kolb’s experiential
learning theory [48], creating structured reflective prompts grounded in its stages. To evaluate ReflecToMeet , we conducted a
five-day user study with three collaborative groups, comparing structured reflection (fact-based or regular and thought-based
or deeper) with unstructured reflection. We analyzed the quality of reflections across Kolb’s learning dimensions, the overall

Related Works
2.1
Reflection in Meetings
Reflection has been defined in multiple ways [7, 10, 11] and is often described as a meta-cognitive activity of examining
one’s own thinking [85]. It is commonly presented as a staged process [4, 27] involving noticing (developing awareness),
understanding (analyzing from multiple perspectives), and acting (shifting perspectives or intentions for the future) [46].
Schön’s Theory of Reflective Practice distinguishes between reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action [88]. The former
occurs during an activity, involving spontaneous thinking and adaptation in real time, whereas the latter takes place after an
activity, involving deliberate consideration of what happened and why to generate insights for future practice [28, 33, 41].
Their respective advantages and limitations have been widely discussed [15, 21, 73, 104]. Building on this literature, our work
focuses on reflection-on-action, with particular emphasis on two segments: deeper or thought-based, and regular or fact-based
reflection.
Research has examined how reflection-on-action and feedback systems shape meeting dynamics and outcomes across
in-person, text-based, and video-conferencing settings [13, 24, 38, 45, 60, 68, 79]. Reflection supports cooperative work by
supporting members to review how they collaborated and what responsibilities they assumed [32], while feedback enables
them to learn from mistakes and track progress, skills, and underlying beliefs [99, 109]. Goal clarification has been identified
as central, as clear goals help participants detect when meetings drift off track and reprioritize [17]. Participants in one
study requested actionable reminders before meetings and feedback tools that incorporate highlights from prior sessions to
support goal-setting [83], while others showed that feedback after meetings improved participation balance and awareness of
communication behaviors [84]. Interactive platforms that visualize discussion or enable collaborative annotations further
enhance engagement, non-verbal participation, and recall [2, 16, 55].
Building on these insights, recent systems have aimed to enhance meeting effectiveness by facilitating intentionality and
reducing organizational tension [91], moderating negative behaviors and supporting leaders [35], incorporating organizational
artifacts such as linked presentations and personalized recaps [3], and deploying AI assistants to help users articulate meeting
purposes and challenges [90]. AI support has also been shown to influence group decision-making quality and shape how
teams perceive their collaborative processes [18]. While these studies illustrate how AI tools and interactive platforms can
support reflection and engagement, they primarily emphasize quick feedback, balanced participation, or visualization of group
interactions. Our work extends this space by integrating reflection-on-action prompts with mechanisms for sharing current
progress, helping individuals reflect on past meetings, stay motivated, remain aligned, and sustain momentum between
sessions.
2.2
Learning with Reflection
Learning with reflection has been widely explored in prior studies. While AI-driven e-learning platforms often personalize
content selection, they are frequently criticized for lacking transparency and user control. Research showed that combining
learner control with visual explanations can be operationalized within such platforms to better support adolescents’ learning
experiences [76]. In addition, hybrid tutoring, which combines human tutors and AI-driven systems, can be effectively
enhanced with LLMs by providing conversational assistance that also facilitates meaningful caregiver involvement [105].

Studies showed that it is possible to detect student reflection on complex problems in virtual learning environments; however,
the suitability of models may vary depending on students’ prior domain experience [86].Students became more aware in the
moment, using this awareness to prepare for and inform their later reflection-on-action [52]. Likewise, leveraging LLMs to
simulate disengaged students allow tutors to practice teaching strategies in a safe environment that mimics real teaching
scenarios, enabling them to learn and apply useful strategies through the system [78]. Reflection were proven to significantly
improve objective task understanding and resulted in more proactive information acquisition behaviors during task completion
[118].
The design of reflective learning experiences is often grounded in Experiential Learning Theory (ELT) proposed by
David Kolb, which conceptualizes learning as a cyclical process of concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract
conceptualization, and active experimentation [47]. Similarly, Constructivist Learning Theory emphasizes that learners
actively construct knowledge through interaction and reflection [81], while Social Constructivism underscores the role of
social interaction and collaborative meaning-making in this process [107]. These theories provide a foundation for integrating
reflection into digital learning environments,
While several studies revealed the positive effects of collaborative learning on academic performance, motivation, cognition,
and social skill development [39, 100], the use of AI to support collaborative learning was also explored from multiple aspects
too [31, 63, 67, 69, 72, 77, 82, 101]. For instance, study showed that a collaborative software development practice named pair
programming in which two programmers work together at one workstation, alternating roles as driver and navigator, can
be more effective if integrated with AI [116]. Another form of collaborative learning, classroom debating can be integrated
with LLM-based AI. Study showed that this type of integration can help groups build consensus, filter information, deepen
understanding, and develop diverse perspectives, thereby enhancing team collaboration [120]. Again, collaborative groups
of students that engaged in deeper, more cohesive, and explanatory interactions were found to learn more effectively and
performed significantly better on post-tests [25]. Moreover, researchers also examined ways to scale up the non-personalized
components of apprentice-style research, while still maintaining the value of personalized one-on-one mentoring [74], to
expand research training opportunities by supporting research communities of practice without increasing faculty mentoring
resources [117].
Overall, these studies show that both individual learning and group collaboration can be facilitated by reflection, especially
when supported by AI systems that make learning more interactive and adaptive. Our work further explores how structured
reflections based on Kolb’s theory can be designed to deepen understanding and to sustain collaborative progress across team
settings.
2.3
Intelligent systems and Human-Centered AI in Collaborative Work
Numerous researchers have investigated how intelligent systems and human-centered AI can enhance collaborative work
[37, 44, 93, 95]. While intelligent systems have been shown to improve both learners’ domain knowledge and collaboration
skills [64], they can also function as cooperative systems that provide intelligent assistance in shared workspaces [102]. One
such system was designed in a virtual whiteboard setting, where each group of students was given a shared space to exchange
documents, add annotations, and use drawing or text tools. Alongside this, the system provided dynamic feedback on their
collaboration, guided by a communication skills model [1].
Building on this foundation, researchers have explored how human-centered AI can facilitate more effective collaboration
by increasing transparency, trust, and user agency. Studies have mapped the design space of human-centered AI, identifying
key challenges and principles for supporting human-AI teamwork [57]. Providing people with concise descriptions of AI
behavior was shown to help them anticipate system limitations and collaborate more effectively [6]. Other work examined how
varied scaffolding levels influence collaboration when co-writing with language models, revealing how different AI support
levels shape team dynamics and perceptions of agency [59]. Similarly, a framework for human-AI co-creation emphasized the
importance of flexible roles and control handoffs during collaborative processes [12].
Researchers have also studied human-AI collaboration in structured teamwork contexts. Human-robot collaboration
research within HCI has highlighted the need to align roles, expectations, and coordination mechanisms when integrating
intelligent agents into teams [92]. AI systems have been explored as potential teammates, with studies showing that their
inclusion can alter group processes, improve outcomes, and create new forms of interdependence [119]. Human-centered
training strategies have been proposed to help teams prepare for such collaborations, clarifying expectations and facilitating
trust in AI partners [62]. Further work has examined collaborative analysis systems, showing how thoughtfully designed

In design-oriented domains, intelligent systems have been used to support collaborative ideation and creativity. AI-driven

AI-assisted reflection based system can be used to enhance engagement, sustain team alignment and progress in collaborative
tasks.
3
Formative Study
3.1
Procedure
To explore how students work together in peer-based meetings where no formal hierarchy exists, we conducted a formative
study examining two central aspects of collaboration. The first focus was on momentum, task distribution, and task management
between meetings, looking at how participants carried forward ideas and tracked progress while working independently. The
second focus was on preparedness and collaboration around meetings, examining how participants organized themselves
between sessions, initiated discussions, and navigated challenges or gaps that emerged when working as equals.
For our formative study, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 10 student participants (3 female, 7 male), aged
20 to 37 years (𝑀= 28.9, 𝑆𝐷= 5.90). Participants were recruited from various U.S. universities through email outreach and
convenience sampling, and at the time of the study, all were enrolled in either undergraduate or graduate programs. All
participants had prior experience completing collaborative tasks such as course projects, research papers, workshops, or poster
presentations at least three times during their academic careers. The study received approval from the authors’ Institutional
Review Board (IRB) office. Interviews were carried out online via Google Meet by the first author and on an average took
27.4 minutes to complete. In total, the dataset included approximately 4.7 hours of recordings and about 27,000 words of
transcribed text. Each participant was compensated with USD $15. The interview questionnaire is provided in Appendix A.
3.2
Data Analysis
We analyzed the interview data using grounded theory. Following grounded theory principles [43, 103], the first and second
authors independently performed open coding, allowing themes to emerge inductively rather than being imposed from
the interview guide. Through ongoing discussions and iterative refinement, they arrived at a shared thematic framework.
Inter-rater reliability for the initial coding was assessed with Cohen’s Kappa, yielding a score of 0.882, which indicates strong
agreement.
3.3
Findings
From our formative study, we have derived three key themes which we have elaborated in the following part of this section.
3.3.1
Temporal Drift and Visible Loss of Momentum.
50% of the participants (N=5) described a recurring pattern in their motivation. They felt a strong boost immediately after a
meeting. This motivation gradually declined until the next meeting or an approaching deadline. Several participants said the
initial lift came from feeling closer to their goal after aligning with others. Over time, this feeling faded as the goal seemed
further away. This pattern reflects the brief “fresh start” effect that often follows temporal landmarks [22]. One participant
(F4) said, “Immediately after a meeting my focus is high; within a couple of days it declines noticeably.” Another (F9) noted,
“When there isn’t a deadline coming up, I tend to lose momentum.”.

Participants (N=6) often linked their motivation to seeing “visible progress” between meetings. They stayed motivated
when they could notice small steps forward. These steps could be because of their own initiatives or their teammates’. Many
said they tried to track each other’s progress in chat groups. But updates often got lost in long threads. One participant
said, “Messages disappear quickly. When you can’t see what’s new, it’s easy to lose steam.” Some participants tried tools like
Jira and Trello. But they were not very enthusiastic about using them. They felt there was no strong reason to commit. F1
said, “There’s no one enforcing it. We’re not in an office, so it’s easy to bend the rules.” Another participant, F3, emphasized the
need for personal responsibility when working with peers. They noted that without individual effort, it becomes hard to stay
organized. As they explained, “When you’re working with friends, you have to balance being chill and staying strict. But if I
know my friend is making progress, it really motivates me to do my part too.”
The pattern was not universal. A few participants (N=3) said they stayed motivated without reminders. Their roles kept
them in structured pipelines where progress was tracked and others relied on their output. In contrast, most participants
preferred having light structure to stay on track. They found that small supports helped them stay aligned with the goal.
These included a weekly summary of progress, a few timely reminders, and a brief recap to reconnect goals with actions.
3.3.2
Pre-Meeting Readiness and Declining Confidence.
Although most of the meetings involved peers and did not have immediate deadlines, participants still reported feeling a sense
of dissatisfaction with the effort they put into their assigned task. This did not always result from visible underperformance,
but rather from the inability to organize their thoughts in a structured way. A few (N=3) noted that they had brainstormed
ideas earlier in the week or mentally rehearsed possible directions, but later forgot these insights entirely. One participant,
F7, said, “I had a great idea when I was in the bus. I thought I’d remember it. Then I couldn’t find it again when I needed it.”
Others mentioned feeling unsure about what they had actually committed to, especially when the work was loosely defined
or open-ended.
As meetings approached, several participants (N=4) described a quiet pressure that made it harder to return to their assigned
tasks. They often began the week with a plan, but daily routines and competing responsibilities crowded their attention. For
many, the issue was not a lack of intention but the difficulty of carrying it out. Some felt that if they had small prompts or
guiding questions to help them re-engage, they might have started earlier and with more clarity. F1 explained, “The task is not
huge, but I keep worrying about how to start. The more I wait, the heavier it feels.” This often led to procrastination, with work
pushed to the night before the meeting. F10 noted, “I open my notes the night before and try to make sense of it. It feels rushed,
like I am only preparing to avoid embarrassment.” Competing priorities also made it difficult to stay on track. As F10 added, “I
want to sit and work on it, but other things keep coming up. At the end of the week, the task feels like the one I have never touched.”
Several participants emphasized that their confidence going into a meeting was shaped less by how much they had
completed and more by how mentally organized they felt. If they had not recently reviewed what had changed or where the
group was headed, it became harder to speak clearly about their own part. In these cases, even basic questions, such as what
their contribution was meant to be, felt mentally taxing. F7 shared, “Sometimes I just don’t know how to explain where I am
with the task. It’s all in pieces in my head.”
3.3.3
Collaboration Hurdles and Meeting Traction.
Some participants (N=4) described imbalance in how work was shared. Some members carried heavier responsibilities because
they did not want tasks left unfinished, while others delayed their contributions until the last moment. This unevenness was
less about ability or willingness and more about habits of timing and reliance on peers. Friendship norms made it difficult
to confront fairness directly, discouraging critical feedback: “Sometimes I do more because I can’t leave it unfinished.”-F9. F5
also talked about this issue, “It is hard to know if everyone is putting in effort, and you do not want to bring it up when you are
all friends on regular basis.” This led to a pattern of quiet acceptance, where disparities in effort were recognized but rarely
addressed.
Beyond workload distribution, participants (N=4) pointed to challenges in the flow of collaboration. Misaligned expectations
about standards and styles, when not identified early, often led to misunderstandings or rework due to poor communication.
As one participant (F6) explained, “I spent hours adding details, but later my teammate said they only wanted a rough draft,
so all that work went to waste.” Under-preparedness and incorrect assumptions also disrupted progress, as teams struggled
to maintain clarity during meetings, which prolonged task completion. Meeting again without coordinated progress often
pushed teams into repeated brainstorming, revisiting the same points rather than moving work forward. As F6 further noted,
“When every week feels like we are having the same discussion, nothing moves ahead unless everyone is on the same page.”

Participants (N=5) also identified broader challenges that align with findings from prior research. One recurring issue was
difficulty maintaining focus during meetings, particularly in remote or home-based settings where distractions are common
[14, 56]. Participants described efforts to remain engaged and avoid missing key points [55], yet noted that conversations often
drifted away from the central agenda [17]. Another concern involved the efficient capture of meeting notes, which participants
viewed as essential for supporting asynchronous engagement at both individual and group levels [111]. Additionally, the
phenomenon of groupthink was observed, where participants tended to converge on simpler ideas, limiting exploration
and innovation. This dynamic was often reinforced by a reluctance to express disagreement or by deferring to more vocal
members of the group [58]. Such dynamics reduced participation, reinforced uneven contributions, and left quieter members
less engaged [83, 84]. Prior work has also shown that in larger groups, talk time often concentrates in a few voices while others
contribute less, and that frequent interruptions or context switching around meetings disrupt focus and require recovery time
[80].
3.3.4
Summary.
The findings of the formative study suggest that students’ motivation followed a temporal rhythm, often increasing immediately
after meetings but declining in the absence of deadlines [98]. This pattern underscores the importance of lightweight structures
that help sustain engagement across intervals. Visible progress, whether through individual steps or group advances, emerged
as a critical factor for maintaining momentum. However, participants noted that such progress was often difficult to observe,
making it harder for them to stay engaged and coordinate efforts effectively. Simple summaries and timely cues may therefore
provide more effective continuity than reliance on complex task management systems. Readiness for meetings was shaped
less by the volume of work completed and more by participants’ sense of mental organization, indicating that reflective
reminders can strengthen both clarity and confidence. Collaboration was further challenged by uneven effort and misaligned
expectations, which reduced productivity and often stalled progress. While broader behaviors such as procrastination and
role-specific work habits extend beyond the immediate scope of our current work, the overall results highlight visible progress,
timely reflection, and minimal preparatory supports as central elements for sustaining collaboration between meetings.
3.4
Design Considerations
Based on our formative study, we propose two design considerations (DCs) to guide the development of an AI-assisted system
aimed at supporting collaborative tasks. The goals focus on strengthening preparedness between meetings, thereby reducing
stress and minimizing misalignment during group interactions.
3.4.1
DC1: Provide personalized reflective prompts and summaries to support preparedness.
Participants often struggled to recall earlier ideas or commitments as meetings approached, which reduced clarity and
confidence. Some described forgetting insights that emerged during the week, while others noted that tasks felt heavier as
they waited longer to re-engage. These lapses often led to last minute preparation and hindered meaningful contributions
during meetings. Providing reflective prompts and concise summaries can help participants recall prior insights, reconnect
with ongoing goals, and translate plans into concrete actions. Personalized prompts are particularly valuable, as they would
encourage participants to reflect on their goals with greater sincerity and to plan more effectively for the upcoming meeting
in relation to both prior discussions and the time elapsed since the last session. DC1 highlights the role of such prompts and
summaries in strengthening preparedness and ensuring that meetings build on prior progress.
3.4.2
DC2: Share team members’ progress to help participants remain focused and informed.
A recurring challenge was the difficulty of staying aware of teammates’ progress between meetings. Without clear visibility,
participants often felt uncertain about the group’s overall status, which reduced motivation and made collaboration feel
uneven. In contrast, visible progress from peers was described as motivating and encouraging reciprocal effort. Beyond
task updates, participants also valued understanding how team members approached the same task differently, including
the level of enthusiasm or effort they were investing. Hearing such perspectives in their own words was seen as a way to
strengthen accountability and increase a sense of collective responsibility. Providing updates in a clear and accessible format,
as emphasized in DC2, can help participants stay focused, reinforce accountability, and minimize misunderstandings about
task status.

4
ReflecToMeet
Based on the design guidelines identified in the formative study, we designed, developed, and empirically evaluated ReflecToMeet
, a AI-assisted system that provides personalized reflective questions and shares team members’ reflective progress to enhance
collaborative preparedness and task involvement between meetings.
4.1
System Overview
ReflecToMeet is an AI-assisted system that provides personalized reflective questions to individuals based on task context and
user responses. It also shares team members’ responses to these reflective questions with one another, allowing participants
to understand each other’s state of progress and individual approaches to shared tasks. ReflecToMeet is designed to address
DC1 and DC2, enabling us to examine whether personalized reflection and shared peer progress can enhance collaborative
preparedness and increase task involvement between meetings.

4.1.1
Theoretical Baseline of ReflecToMeet .
The reflective questions in ReflecToMeet are based on Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory [48]. This theory explains learning
as a continuous cycle that involves four stages:
• Concrete Experience (CE): Engaging in a new experience or situation.
• Reflective Observation (RO): Thinking back and reflecting on the experience.
• Abstract Conceptualization (AC): Forming theories, ideas, or generalizations from those reflections.
• Active Experimentation (AE): Testing those ideas in practice, which leads to new experiences.
This theory conceptualizes learning as a cyclical process of concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract concep-
tualization, and active experimentation, through which knowledge emerges from the transformation of experience. This
framework highlights the importance of designing systems and activities that cause reflection and iteration, enabling learners
to connect lived experiences with theoretical insights and apply them in practice.
We used four stages of Kolb’s theory to design reflective questions that would encourage users to think about their work in
a structured way. As per our design consideration, DC1, we hypothesized that reflective questions would help users recall
their commitments and prepare for their tasks more effectively. These reflections first guide users to recall and reflect on
what happened in the previous meeting (CE, RO). Then they ask users to interpret these experiences and identify broader
insights (AC). Finally, they encourage users to apply these insights in preparing for the upcoming meeting (AE). Each reflective

question is adapted to the specific task context and is informed from a summary of all prior interactions, including the user’s
earlier responses. The reflective questions are supposed to appear regularly in the time interval between two meetings and
are designed to connect one meeting to the next. After each new meeting, a new set of reflective questions is generated so
that the cycle of reflection and preparation continues.
In line with DC2, we further hypothesized that sharing responses among teammates would promote awareness of collective
progress and facilitate greater task engagement. Users are notified when their peers’ responses become available in the system,
allowing them to view each other’s reflections as an evolving work in progress.

4.1.2
System Development. ReflecToMeet was implemented as a web-based system to support structured reflection between
meetings. The front-end was developed in Streamlit, providing participants with secure login and an interface for uploading
meeting transcripts periodically. Uploaded transcripts were processed through GPT-4o-mini API calls, which generated
meeting summaries and extracted key discussion points to serve as inputs for subsequent reflective questions. The backend
used MongoDB to store user data. Nginx managed routing, WebSocket upgrades, and HTTPS enforcement across the system.
As shown in Figure 2, the workflow begins when a user uploads a meeting transcript to ReflecToMeet (indicated with a
dotted line). The system processes the transcript using GPT-4o-mini to create a comprehensive meeting summary (CMS),
a short recap, and a set of personalized reflective questions. The recap is a brief note, limited to 150 words, that reminds
participants of the previous discussion and their ongoing task. The CMS is updated after each meeting to include, meeting’s
state of progress, any changes, or clarifications. The reflective questions, designed around Kolb’s experiential learning cycle,
guide participants in reviewing their work and preparing for the next step. Users respond to these questions, and their answers
are stored for later reflections. The system then combines the CMS with the reflective responses to maintain continuity in
generating reflective questions for the following day. Meeting transcript is added only on days when meetings take place, and
the same cycle continues into the next interval. The following text shows a sample recap generated using the prompt shown
in Appendix B.1.
User1 began his collaboration by focusing on defining LLM hallucinations, identifying their causes, and summarizing
related works on mitigation strategies. He drafted a definition section and categorized research into taxonomy,
evaluation benchmarks, and mitigation techniques. The work division was structured around clarity of themes, which
enhanced focus and efficiency; however, it also posed risks of overlap or gaps without proper coordination. Throughout
the reflections, User1 emphasized the importance of maintaining communication to ensure all sections aligned and
complemented each other, leading to a more cohesive understanding of the topic. This structured approach allowed
for substantial progress in organizing complex information related to LLM challenges and solutions.

4.1.3
Interaction Workflow.
While designing ReflecToMeet , we aimed for a minimal and easy-to-navigate interface structured around two main tabs:
Dashboard and Reflections. After each meeting, participants access the Reflections tab to upload the meeting transcript and
input basic task-related information such as the task name and assigned responsibilities. Based on this input, the system
generates a set of personalized reflective questions, which the participant responds to directly within the interface. For the
user study, we used two set of structured reflective questions: (1) regular, fact-based questions and (2) deeper, thought-based
questions. Regular reflections were designed to mainly ask “what” questions that encourage participants to reconsider or
review their actions. It involved brief, structured check-ins that help participants recall meeting goals, track progress, and
identify immediate next steps. It emphasized consistency and accountability without adding a lot of cognitive load. In contrast,
deeper reflections were designed to ask “how” and “why” questions that encourage thoughtfulness and deeper analysis
(shown in Table 2). Deeper reflection went even further by prompting participants to critically examine their strategies,
challenges, and assumptions. It encouraged exploring alternative approaches, connecting current tasks to broader goals,
and generating new ideas to facilitate confidence, teamwork, and long-term growth, though with greater effort required. In
addition, we used one set of unstructured reflective questions which are shown in Table 3.
Once the user submitted their response to the reflective questions, the response appears on the Dashboard, alongside the
question, previous responses, and reflections from team members answering the reflective question on the same day. To
maintain engagement between meetings, the system sends email notifications when a new reflective question is generated or
when a teammate responds, reminding participants to revisit the interface and remain connected with their own and their
team’s evolving understanding of the task. The interaction flow of ReflecToMeet is shown in Figure 2.

Methodology: User Study
To evaluate the impact of ReflecToMeet on collaborative tasks, we conducted a user study in which participants used our
AI-based system to complete a shared task. The primary goal of ReflecToMeet is not to track progress, but to support reflection
on individual actions while maintaining focus on the overall objectives. It also aims to enhance self-efficacy by allowing
users to stay informed about their teammates’ reflective thoughts. To support this, the system design incorporates Kolb’s
experiential learning framework, introducing varying levels of reflection to assess how users perceive them and whether these
differences influence their experience individually and collectively. The following section describes the user study conducted
with ReflecToMeet in detail.
5.1
Study Design and Setup
We conducted a user study using ReflecToMeet , where participants were paired to design a poster over a period of five days.
We selected a task that required real collaboration over multiple days to better evaluate our system’s impact. Participants were
allowed to form pairs based on their own preference. Those who did not select a partner were randomly paired. All participants
were given clear instructions about the task. They were asked to hold two online meetings. The first meeting was to choose a
poster topic and divide responsibilities. We provided suggested topics, such as reducing food waste, improving study habits,
making online learning more engaging, or cutting down screen time, but participants were free to choose their own topics.
The second meeting was held to review and finalize the poster. Each meeting was recommended to last no longer than 40
minutes. Participants were instructed to use any online meeting platform they preferred and to transcribe their meeting data
using any tool into a text file to upload to ReflecToMeet . The entire study was conducted remotely. We instructed participants
to refrain from using any LLM tools for this task. Between these two meetings, participants received daily notifications to
answer reflective questions within the system. On each day, they could also view their partner’s reflections to stay informed.
Communication outside the scheduled meetings was restricted to reduce unstructured interaction and isolate the effect of
guided reflection. The study focused on how structured reflection shaped the individual’s collaborative experience, rather
than evaluating the quality of the final poster.

5.2
Participants and Group Assignment
We recruited 30 students for this study, including 18 male and 12 female participants, with ages ranging from 20 to 34
years (𝑀= 26.40, 𝑆𝐷= 3.87). Participants were enrolled in undergraduate or graduate programs at various universities in
the United States. Recruitment was carried out through convenience and snowball sampling methods. All participants had
prior experience completing collaborative academic tasks, such as course projects, research papers, workshops, or poster
presentations, at least twice in their academic or professional career. We did not include participants from the formative study
to avoid bias and ensure that all participants approached the task with fresh perspectives. Participants had to spent around
4 hours during the entire study in the span of 5 days. Each participant received USD $50 as compensation. The study was
approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the authors’ institution.

5.3
Study Timeline and Reflection Flow
In real settings, meetings may occur at different intervals such as weekly, biweekly, or monthly. For this empirical study, we
limited the reflection stage to five consecutive days to balance task load, sustain participant engagement, and keep the study
duration manageable. During this period, participants followed a structured five-stage plan. The approach could be extended
in longer studies by introducing gaps between reflections, by spacing reflections around major milestones, or by aligning
them with project deadlines. These adjustments would allow the structure to adapt to longer timelines while preserving the
benefits of staged reflection. In Table 1, we showed the stages divided across five days based on Kolb’s four learning stages.
On Day #1, participants held their first meeting, and they received their reflective questions focused on reflection after
this initial meeting. On Day #2, the questions were designed to check early progress and identify challenges in an early
stage. Day #3 marked the midpoint of the task, and the focus shifted to aligning with teammates and reflecting on individual
contributions. On Day #4, the day before the final meeting, the questions emphasized preparation by outlining the final steps

unstructured reflective questions. All groups received the same instructions regarding the timing of these tasks, which were

and review their partner’s responses. Participants in G3 were not shown or notified about their partner’s reflections. From

and poster design were completed, participants submitted their last meeting transcript along with the completed poster.

5.4
Evaluation Method
We designed a post-task feedback survey to evaluate the impact of ReflecToMeet on participants’ collaborative experience.
The survey was grounded in the same theoretical framework that guided the design of our reflective questions, drawing
on Kolb’s experiential learning cycle (Concrete Experience, Reflective Observation, Abstract Conceptualization, and Active
Experimentation). Most of the questions (5-point Likert, 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) targeted the perceived
effects of reflection at each stage: recall and presence during the task (CE), awareness and interpretation of experiences (RO),
drawing connections and generalizations (AC), and planning future actions (AE). We tested deeper reflection questions for
all four stages, while regular reflection questions were used for three stages (CE, RO, AE). Additional questions measured
participants’ overall sense of preparedness, confidence, and perceived usefulness of the reflection process. We also included
questions about the timing, frequency, and delivery method of the reflective prompts, teammate reflection awareness for
G1/G2, and a few open-ended questions to capture qualitative insights on perceived benefits, design feedback, and suggestions
for future use. In addition, we administered the NASA-TLX to capture perceived workload. Participants rated the six sub-scales
(Mental Demand, Physical Demand, Temporal Demand, Performance, Effort, and Frustration) on 1–10 scales. These workload
ratings were analyzed alongside the survey outcomes.
We piloted the survey with a small sample to ensure clarity and reasonable length; participants took approximately 10–12
minutes to complete it. Following this, we administered the final version at the end of the study. The responses were analyzed
using a mixed-method approach, which is described in detail in the Results section.

We adopted a mixed-method analysis to evaluate the results of our study. First, we report findings from the quantitative

6.1
Quantitative Analysis
We analyzed three independent conditions: regular reflection (G1), deeper reflection (G2), and a control without reflection (G3),
with 𝑛= 10 participants per group. The results were assessed using six subscales derived from Kolb’s learning cycle (CE-R,
CE-D, RO-R, RO-D, AC-R, AE-R, AE-D) and an Overall Effect composite included feeling prepared for the second meeting,
increased confidence in contributing, greater awareness of the work process, and willingness to recommend the reflection
approach (structured for G1/G2; unstructured for G3). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale and aggregated into the
respective subscales. Internal consistency for the two-item subscales was estimated using Pearson’s 𝑟and the two-item 𝛼,
while the composite was evaluated with Cronbach’s 𝛼.
Given the ordinal measurement scale and small sample sizes, we employed rank-based nonparametric inference consistent
with prior CHI practice [5, 26]. Specifically, we used the Kruskal–Wallis test for three-group comparisons, reporting effect
sizes (𝜖2), and conducted Games–Howell post hoc tests when appropriate. For pairwise comparisons, we also report Cliff’s
𝛿as a measure of effect size. Reliability analysis indicated that RO-R demonstrated strong internal consistency (𝛼= 0.88),
while AC-D, AE-D, and RO-D showed good reliability (𝛼= 0.72–0.77). CE-D, CE-R, and AE-R showed moderate consistency
(𝛼= 0.50–0.65), and the Overall Effect factor exhibited borderline reliability (𝛼= 0.57). Table 4 and Figure 5 represent the
result of quantitative data analysis.

greater variability. These results suggest that participants generally agreed that peer reflections were useful and motivating.

6.2
Qualitative Analysis
To gain deeper insight into participants’ experiences, we included several open-ended questions in the post-study survey.
These questions asked about the usability of the interface, the usefulness of reflection in supporting task planning and
collaboration, reactions to receiving two types of questions daily (for Group 2), and the influence of viewing a teammate’s
reflections. Participants also described what worked or did not in their second meeting and offered suggestions for improving
the reflection model. We analyzed the responses using grounded theory [43, 103], allowing themes to emerge inductively
from the data. The first and second authors independently performed open coding, followed by iterative discussion to develop
a shared thematic framework. Inter-rater reliability for the initial coding was strong (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.861), indicating a
high level of agreement.

asked for clearer cues. As a regular reflection participant (G1), GP7 mentioned:

As Group 2 received both fact-based and thought-based reflection prompts, their responses offer insight into how this
dual-question format shaped their experience. Most participants (N = 7) appreciated the combination, noting that it offered a
balance between maintaining task focus and encouraging deeper insight. Fact-based prompts were considered helpful for
clarifying goals, tracking progress, and reducing procrastination. Because they were easy to answer, participants found them
effective for sustaining momentum across the week.
“The factual question helped me remember my progress and keep procrastination in check... The thought-provoking
one helped me see the difficulties of the task more clearly... Overall, the approach worked well by covering both
aspects.” -DP5
In contrast, thought-based prompts required more cognitive effort but supported sense-making by helping participants

“On busy days, the deeper prompt felt a bit heavy, though it often led to useful reflection when I had the time.” -DP7
This structure helped participants stay organized without becoming overwhelmed, aligning their daily task flow with
opportunities for deeper learning.

7
Discussion
Our findings show that reflective cues supported participants in recalling daily activities, as indicated by the absence of
significant differences in recall-related scores (CE-R, CE-D) across groups. This suggests that both structured and unstructured
reflection acted as retrieval cues that stabilized memory but did not change how participants described their work, consistent
with prior findings that reflection often produces descriptive accounts rather than “deeper reframing” [19]. Clearer differences
emerged on interpretive dimensions: deeper reflection enhanced noticing on simpler items, while regular reflection sometimes
matched or exceeded it on more demanding cues. The effectiveness of reflective questions thus depended as much on timing
and workload as on depth [34], with deeper questions burdensome under workload but valuable at natural pauses. Both
reflective groups outperformed the control in spotting patterns and making sense of past work, comparable to the recap tools
that transform raw notes into usable summaries [90, 114]. The strongest benefits of deeper reflection appeared in planning for
future work, aligning with research on self-regulation, intentions, and the motivational effects of temporal landmarks [22].
Deeper reflection encourages more thorough observation of a topic, which can be especially valuable for tasks that unfold
over a longer period. In contrast, its benefits are harder to realize in short-term projects, where the added cognitive effort may
outweigh the gains. However, it is important to note that this user study was conducted in a compressed time-frame, where
all groups had to respond to reflective questions regularly, which naturally felt more cognitively burdensome. Overall, these
findings reinforce Kolb’s experiential learning model, which progresses from concrete experience to reflective observation,
abstract conceptualization, and preparation for action. In the following section, we discuss the various aspects of the design
implications of ReflecToMeet :
7.1
Time, Personalization and Frequency
As per our findings from the user study, timing, personalization, and frequency emerged as central factors shaping how
participants engaged with reflection. Reflection were seen as useful when they remained brief and factual, supporting quick
recall without interrupting ongoing work. Deeper reflection, by contrast, carried more weight which led to cognitive load.
Probably, deeper reflections are best received at a certain interval, like mid-cycle or just before meetings, when participants
naturally would pause to consolidate progress. This suggests that reflection is not only about what is asked but when it is
asked, and at what level of cognitive demand [50, 75]. Yet this insight surfaces a tricky tension: at what point does a “helpful
reminder” turn into a distraction rather than a prompt for reflection? Because reflection is a personal and internal cognitive
process, it is appropriate that the individual makes the ultimate decision. Moreover, personalization is shaped not only by
contextual factors but also by the person’s own schedule [19, 49, 61]. Allowing participants to set their own reflection windows
and delivery channels (for example, evening reminders or calendar notifications) will increase flexibility and compliance.
However, while personal choice supports autonomy, it also risks undermining team rhythm.

While participants in our study chose to do steady daily engagement, in real life scenario there could be different circum-
stance where someone may lean toward bursts of concentrated work. Designing for both styles suggests the value of features
like weekly syntheses that allow “burst workers” to stay aligned without daily entries. Yet, in accommodating both rhythms,
systems risk implicitly privileging one productivity ethic over another, framing reflection either as a discipline of consistency
or as a flexible, on-demand resource [9, 70]. A similar tension appeared when managing mental effort. Participants valued
features like adjusting question depth or saving answers to finish later, since these made reflection easier. Yet too much
structure can feel controlling. For example, if every prompt always asks “why” or if sample answers push users to copy a
certain style, the tool risks telling people how to think instead of letting them reflect in their own way.
Sustaining engagement over time required variety. Participants appreciated when regular and deeper reflections felt
personalized and when there was a day-long gap between two reflections. They also suggested adjusting the frequency of
reflection based on how much they responded. These strategies may reduce fatigue and keep reflection fresh. Yet adaptive
reflection raises concerns. Does adjusting frequency based on behavior truly respect individual choice, or could it quietly
encourage avoidance or limit opportunities for growth? This question is especially relevant in academic and peer groups, where
participation is voluntary but contributions also carry social weight. Another issue is that when responding to reflections
feels demanding, skipping them can also create pressure since absence itself becomes noticeable. Participants emphasized the
need for a gentle way back after missed days, such as a single catch-up summary that eased guilt and lowered the effort to
re-engage. While this approach reduces friction, it raises an important question: should missed reflections be viewed as a
sign of overload, suggesting the system should ease off, or as a lapse that requires correction? The way absence is addressed
strongly influences whether reflection is experienced as supportive or as an imposed obligation. These findings suggest that
systems like ReflecToMeet must balance support with flexibility, allowing reflection to remain both effective and self-directed.
7.2
From Reflection to Accountability
Participants noted the value of visibility when it focused on practical coordination between meetings. Seeing which tasks
were in motion after a meeting, where blockers had emerged, and how work depended on others helped them plan their next
steps for the following session. They also suggested using metrics such as scoreboards or streaks to quantify or visualize
progress. Although this might make tracking easier on a regular basis, it can also create pressure to perform rather than
collaborate, shifting the purpose of reflection from genuine progress to mere appearances. The challenge is to provide just
enough visibility to coordinate without encouraging performative reporting. One participant (RP9) noted that their teammate
was not very engaged or consistent in responding to reflective questions on time. As a result, they suggested a feature that
would allow writing a short note such as “waiting for response from X,” to appear in the shared meeting recap, so that partners
could understand why progress might be slowing. When combined with gentle reciprocity nudges, such context could invite
updates without placing blame and help sustain coordination. At the same time, if a partner consistently under-participates,
visibility may facilitate empathy by clarifying their situation; otherwise, it may instead create frustration and lead others to
disengage. Moreover, making progress visible does not ensure accountability. If someone deliberately misrepresents their
progress and this becomes evident in the next meeting, it can erode trust in the system and reduce team members’ motivation
to engage. Requiring periodic progress reports can help mitigate this risk by making it more difficult to sustain false claims
until everyone has to face consequence.
Participants appreciated when their reflections were converted into simple summaries that specified what needed to
be done and who would take responsibility before the next meeting. For instance, if someone wrote “I need to finish the
introduction,” ReflecToMeet generated a recap that identified the action and the person responsible which then served as a
planning anchor in the following meeting. This process made it easier to move from ideas to concrete tasks and created a
clearer sense of accountability across meetings. However, there is a risk that if the system generates too much of the plan,
participants may feel they are only following instructions rather than making their own decisions. A feature that allows users
to override or edit the system’s summary would therefore be more appropriate. ReflecToMeet was designed and evaluated in
settings without a formal hierarchy. In teams with designated leaders, the system would need to present a clear overview of
risks, delays, and overall progress while protecting the privacy of individual reflections. Designing such a system without
compromising personal space is challenging, since reflection is inherently personal and cannot be fully controlled [29, 87].
Moreover, excessive visibility can create a sense of surveillance. If participants believe that every word is being monitored,
they may withhold honest reflections, undermining the value of the practice itself.
Participants emphasized that privacy is central to reflective practice because entries often contain tentative ideas, uncertainty,
or frustration that are not intended for others and may not yet be ready to share in the next meeting. A prudent default is to

keep all entries private and allow users to promote selected items to partner or team visibility only when they are ready to

only aggregate views and trends, such as overall progress or recurring blockers, and keep raw reflections private unless the
author explicitly opts to share them. This approach prevents reflective writing from becoming part of formal evaluation and
reduces bias in assessment.
Participants suggested adding voice recording features to reduce the need for typing long responses between meetings. Not
everyone is comfortable with writing; some individuals prefer speaking, sketching, or leaving brief audio notes as they prepare
for or reflect on meetings. Supporting multiple modalities such as text, audio, or simple sketches can make reflection more
accessible and inclusive. For example, one participant mentioned recording a 30-second voice note while commuting, which
felt more natural than composing a written entry. By offering diverse input options, systems can avoid privileging one form
of expression over others. Beyond modality, accessibility must also account for users with different physical, cognitive, and
situational needs. Reflection tools should be designed to accommodate this diversity, ensuring that all users can participate
comfortably. Reflections should be delivered in ways that align with users’ cognitive load, providing both ease of response
and a sense of autonomy in the reflective process.
7.3
Generalizability of ReflecToMeet
Our study was conducted over a fixed five-day period, during which participants held two meetings and responded to daily
reflection designed using Kolb’s Experiential Learning Model [48]. Each day’s prompt targeted a different stage of Kolb’s cycle,
helping participants recall recent actions, interpret their experiences, and prepare for the next session. This tightly sequenced
schedule worked because the interval between meetings was short, allowing participants to sustain attention and benefit from
daily reflection without the process feeling disconnected from their ongoing work. However, real-world meetings often occur
on looser cycles such as every seven, ten, or fifteen days. Extending ReflecToMeet to these contexts would require recalibrating
both the frequency and intensity of reflection. If the same five-day structure were applied unchanged across a fifteen-day gap,
participants would face ten unanchored days, likely experiencing drift or forgetting what had been discussed, while asking
for fifteen daily reflections would be excessive and could create fatigue and reduce engagement.
One way to address this is to stretch the Kolb-based structure across longer intervals by adjusting frequency. In a seven-day
interval, the system could retain near-daily check-ins but reduce their cognitive load through short and low-effort reflection
for quick status and planning, while inserting one deeper reflection around midweek to encourage conceptual reframing. In a
ten-day interval, reflections could appear every two to three days, alternating between brief recaps of progress and occasional
deeper questions that synthesize work completed so far. In a fifteen-day interval, the system could adopt a layered structure
with early lightweight reflection on days three to five to keep momentum, mid-cycle deeper reflections on days seven to ten
to revisit assumptions, and pre-meeting reflection on days thirteen to fourteen to consolidate what has been done and plan
what will be discussed (shown in Table 5). This approach preserves the pedagogical intent of Kolb’s model by cycling through
experience, reflection, conceptualization, and planning while spacing it to match longer project rhythms. ReflecToMeet could
implement this adaptively by using meeting dates as anchors and automatically rescaling the reflection schedule between
them.
Beyond rescheduling, longer intervals would also require stronger context persistence. When gaps stretch to two weeks
or more, participants are more likely to forget prior decisions or lose narrative continuity. To support this, ReflecToMeet
would need to generate cumulative summaries that integrate earlier reflections and meeting notes so each prompt reactivates
shared context before asking users to contribute. These adaptations could also support broader deployments beyond our
study setting. In classroom projects spanning several weeks, reflection intensity could ramp up before milestones, while in
research labs or distributed professional teams, low-frequency lightweight reflections could maintain alignment across long
cycles. In mentorship or training programs, deep reflective reflections could appear only at key developmental checkpoints,
separated by long intervals of lighter progress-oriented reflections. Overall, while our five-day structure demonstrated that
daily reflection can sustain short-term momentum, generalizing ReflecToMeet requires decoupling the reflection frequency

Limitations and Future Work
Though ReflecToMeet has shown that it can facilitate better collaborative preparedness through its reflection-on-action based
cues, it is not without limitations. First, this study primarily relied on self-reported measures of collaboration quality and
engagement, and did not evaluate the quality of the posters produced by each group. As a result, it remains unclear whether
improvements in preparedness directly translated into higher-quality outcomes. Future studies will include independent
assessments of final artifacts to triangulate perceived and actual performance. Second, while responding to reflection prompts
requires cognitive effort and time to process, this study was conducted over a compressed five-day span to observe effects
within a controlled time-frame. Such rapid cycles may have amplified cognitive load and may not reflect how participants
would engage with reflection over longer intervals. In future work, we plan to examine how adaptive scheduling and varied
reflection frequency influence engagement and outcomes across longer projects. Third, the reflective structure used in
ReflecToMeet was based on Kolb’s Experiential Learning Model. While this model helped structure reflection in our study, it has
faced criticism for oversimplifying learning processes. More recent work, such as Morris’s experiential learning framework,
offers simplified and context-sensitive adaptations that may better suit diverse learners [71]. In future iterations, we aim to
incorporate revised versions of Kolb’s model and explore alternative frameworks to account for the variability of learning
styles and contexts. Finally, this study was conducted with small, peer-based student teams working on structured tasks in a
non-hierarchical setting. This limits the generalizability of our findings to other contexts. Larger groups often involve more
diverse dynamics, and hierarchical teams may introduce power asymmetries that influence how reflection is perceived and
enacted. Future work will evaluate ReflecToMeet in settings with more heterogeneous team structures, larger group sizes, and
different organizational cultures to examine its scalability and robustness.
9
Conclusion
In collaborative settings, one of the most challenging moments occurs during the long intervals between meetings, when
attention, motivation, and accountability often drift. While prior research has explored AI-assisted support before, during,
and after meetings, these gaps remain underexplored even though they represent a critical period for sustaining collaboration.
To address this gap, we first conducted a formative interview study with students to identify the challenges they face in
maintaining progress during these intervals. Drawing on these findings, we designed an AI-assisted reflection system named
ReflecToMeet that integrates personalized reflective prompts with shared awareness of teammates’ reflections. Our five-day
study with three collaborative groups in different settings showed that ReflecToMeet improved several aspects of collaborative
preparedness: it helped participants stay engaged, reduced drift, and turned reflection into small, manageable checkpoints.
We also present several design implications based on these findings. Overall, our work shows how AI-guided reflection can
support collaboration in a way that feels helpful rather than intrusive. By extending reflection-on-action into the intervals
between meetings, ReflecToMeet helps groups remain engaged and better prepared for future collaboration.
