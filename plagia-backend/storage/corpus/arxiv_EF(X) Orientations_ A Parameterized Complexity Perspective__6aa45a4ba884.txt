Abstract
The concept of fair orientations in graphs was in-
troduced by [Christodoulou et al., 2023], naturally
modeling fair division scenarios in which resources
are only contested by neighbors. In this model, ver-
tices represent agents and undirected edges repre-
sent goods; edges have to be oriented towards one
of their endpoints, i.e., allocated to one of their ad-
jacent agents. Although EFX orientations (envy-
free up to any good) have been extensively studied
in this setting, EF orientations (envy-free) remain
unexplored. In this work, we initiate their study,
mostly under the lens of parameterized complex-
ity, presenting various tractable cases, hardness re-
sults, and parameterizations. Our results concern
both simple graphs and multigraphs. Interestingly,
many of our results transfer to EFX orientations,
thus complementing and improving upon previous
work; notably, we answer an open question regard-
ing the structural parameterized complexity of the
latter problem on graphs of polynomially-bounded
valuations. We also show that EF orientations are
tractable in cases in which EFX orientations are
not, particularly for binary valuations. Lastly, we
consider charity [Caragiannis et al., 2019a] in the
orientation setting, establishing algorithms for find-
ing the minimum amount of edges that have to be
removed from a graph in order for EF(X) orienta-
tions to exist.

Introduction
The fair division of indivisible goods is a fundamental prob-
lem in computer science, with numerous applications, such
as distribution of inheritance, scheduling jobs, and allocating
computational resources to machine learning models. The
concept of fair division of divisible resources was introduced
in [Steihaus, 1948]; in recent years, interest has shifted to-
wards indivisible goods, both due to the aforementioned ap-
plications and due to several open questions surrounding
the study of envy. Since envy-free (EF) allocations are not
guaranteed to exist, research has focused on envy minimiza-
tion (e.g., [Lipton et al., 2004; Nguyen and Rothe, 2014;
Kanellopoulos et al., 2025]), and relaxations of EF, such as
EF1 [Budish, 2010] and EFX [Caragiannis et al., 2019b].
The existence of EFX allocations in the general setting re-
mains a major open question in fair division [Amanatidis et
al., 2023], with some results for 2 [Plaut and Roughgarden,
2020] and 3 agents [Chaudhury et al., 2024; Akrami et al.,
2023], and further work limiting agents’ valuations [Ama-
natidis et al., 2021; HV et al., 2025; Hosseini et al., 2021;
Babaioff et al., 2021].
A restricted version of the problem was introduced by
[Christodoulou et al., 2023], with a graph model where
the vertices represent agents and the edges represent goods;
it was proven in the same paper that EFX allocations al-
ways exist in this restricted version for simple graphs. This
model has been extensively studied since, with the exis-
tence of EFX allocations also being studied in multigraphs
[Afshinmehr et al., 2025; Sgouritsa and Sotiriou, 2025;
Bhaskar and Pandit, 2025; Kaviani et al., 2024].
An in-
teresting concept in this model is that of EFX orientations,
i.e., EFX allocations where edges have to be allocated to one
of their endpoints, naturally modeling practical applications
where resources are contested only by neighbors. EFX ori-
entations have also received significant attention in recent
years, with their parameterized complexity in particular be-
ing studied in [Deligkas et al., 2025; Blazej et al., 2025;
Afshinmehr et al., 2025].
In spite of this, EF orientations remain largely unexplored.
In this work we study them primarily under the lens of pa-
rameterized complexity, and present a plethora of tractable
special cases, parameterized algorithms, and hardness results.
Many of our results transfer to EFX orientations, thus com-
plementing and improving upon results from [Deligkas et al.,
2025; Blazej et al., 2025].
Another interesting relaxation of EF(X) that we consider
in this work concerns allocations in which not every item is
allocated to an agent, i.e., EF(X) with charity [Caragiannis
et al., 2019a]. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first
to consider charity in the graph setting of [Christodoulou et
al., 2023], establishing algorithms for finding the minimum
number of edges that have to remain unoriented (i.e., donated
to charity) in order for EF(X) orientations to exist.

1.1
Related Work
Allocations.
Regarding EF allocations, NP-completeness
and inapproximability results are shown in [Lipton et al.,
2004]. Recent results for the existence of EFX allocations
include the following: [Plaut and Roughgarden, 2020] show
existence for two agents with general monotone valuations;
[Chaudhury et al., 2024] for three agents with additive valu-
ations; [Akrami et al., 2023] improve this to a slightly more
general class; [HV et al., 2025] show existence when agents
have one of three additive valuations.
Orientations.
The
existence
of
EFX
orientations
is
known to be NP-complete even for symmetric valuations
[Christodoulou et al., 2023]. [Deligkas et al., 2025] prove
that the problem remains NP-complete even for graphs with
vertex cover size 8 or multigraphs with 10 vertices, while also
identifying one of the few known fixed-parameter tractable
cases through the parameterization by slim tree-cut width
(defined in [Ganian and Korchemna, 2024]).
[Blazej et
al., 2025] study the complexity of the problem in rela-
tion to how close the graph is to being bipartite, proving
that it remains NP-complete for (a subclass of) binary in-
stances, while also showing a linear-time algorithm for sym-
metric binary instances of constant treewidth. [Hsu, 2024;
Afshinmehr et al., 2025] further study EFX orientations in
(bipartite) multigraphs. [Zeng and Mehta, 2025] relate the
existence of EFX orientations to the graph’s chromatic num-
ber. EFX orientations have also been studied in the setting of
chores, i.e., negatively valued items, and mixed manna, i.e.,
goods and chores [Hsu and King, 2025; Zhou et al., 2024].
Despite all the aforementioned work for EFX orientations, to
the best of our knowledge, the only work relevant to EF orien-
tations appears in [Misra and Sethia, 2024], where the authors
study EF allocations in simple graphs, with a polynomial-
time algorithm for binary valuations also transferring to ori-
entations.
Charity.
The concept of charity was first introduced in
[Caragiannis et al., 2019a], in the setting of EFX allocations.
In [Chaudhury et al., 2021], the number of unallocated items
that guarantees existence of EFX for general monotone valu-
ations was upper bounded by n−1, where n is the number of
agents. This bound was reduced to n −2 for additive valua-
tions [Berger et al., 2022], and was later extended to general
monotone valuations by [Mahara, 2024]. For 4 agents, this
number is 1 [Berger et al., 2022]. To the best of our knowl-
edge, charity has never been studied in the context of fair
orientations prior to this work, although one could obtain a
charity bound of |E|/2 for EFX orientations in simple graphs
by removing edges to obtain a bipartite graph [Erd˝os, 1965],
which is known to always admit an EFX orientation [Zeng
and Mehta, 2025].

Our Contributions
We start by considering in Section 3.1 EF ORIENTATION1 in
multigraphs with binary valuation functions. Our first result
is to show that the problem admits a linear-time algorithm that

ORIENTATION problem (i.e., finding the minimum amount
of edges that have to be removed for an EF orientation to
exist). Towards obtaining this algorithm we prove a structural
theorem (Theorem 5) that roughly says that an EF orientation
exists if and only if each of the graph’s connected components
has at least one among 4 properties. This result contrasts the
NP-hardness of EFX ORIENTATION in the analogous setting,
which persists even for symmetric instances on simple graphs
that are 2 edge-deletions away from being bipartite [Blazej et
al., 2025].
Moving on, in Section 3.2 we present various hardness re-
sults for EF ORIENTATION. First we show that the prob-
lem remains NP-hard even on 3-regular simple graphs with
weights in {1, 2} and symmetric valuations. Given this hard-
ness for very restrictive valuations, we next ask whether re-
stricting the structure of the input graph allows for more effi-
cient algorithms. Via a simple reduction from PARTITION we
show that, for arbitrary weights, EF ORIENTATION remains
NP-hard even for simple graphs of vertex cover number 2 and
multigraphs with 2 vertices. Therefore, we move on to con-
sider instances with polynomially-bounded weights, in which
case we prove W[1]-, XNLP-, and XALP-hardness for the
parameterization by vertex cover, pathwidth, and treewidth
respectively (Theorem 12), thus arguing that the problem
remains highly intractable even in structured inputs; all of
these results hold even for simple symmetric instances. As
a byproduct of our reduction in Theorem 12, we obtain a
no(vc/ log vc) ETH-based lower bound, where vc denotes the
vertex cover number of the input graph.
Although the hardness results of Section 3.2 seem very
discouraging, we next identify restricted cases on which we
are able to obtain fast algorithms.
First, we consider in-
stances on simple graphs with at most k heavy edges, that
is, edges that have valuation at least 2 for an endpoint; this
naturally generalizes the binary-valuation regime considered
in Section 3.1, albeit restricted to simple graphs. Here we
develop a 2knO(1) algorithm, which we complement with a
tight SETH-based lower bound (Theorems 9 and 13). We
view this as a first step towards generalizing the algorithm
of Theorem 6 to an FPT algorithm parameterized by the
number of heavy edges. Second, using dynamic program-
ming (DP), we show that EF ORIENTATION can be solved in
time (n + m)O(tw) for polynomially-bounded weights, where
tw denotes the treewidth of the graph and m the number
of edges. Our algorithm works for the more general MIN-
CHARITY EF ORIENTATION problem in multigraphs with
non-symmetric valuations, and matches, up to a log-factor in
the exponent, the lower bound of Theorem 12.
EF ORIENTATION is arguably a very natural, understud-
ied problem and our results, apart from being interesting in
their own right, additionally pave the way to advance the
state-of-the-art for the closely related, and better understood,
EFX ORIENTATION problem. In particular, in Section 4.1
we present a reduction from EF ORIENTATION to EFX ORI-
ENTATION that allows us to transfer most hardness results
of Section 3.2 to the latter. Consequently, we obtain NP-
hardness for simple graphs of vertex cover number 4 and

multigraphs of 4 vertices, improving upon the hardness by
[Deligkas et al., 2025] for constants 8 and 10 respectively.
More importantly, we obtain a no(vc/ log vc) ETH-based lower
bound for simple symmetric instances with polynomially-
bounded weights.
As a matter of fact, in Section 4.2 we
yet again employ DP and obtain an (n + m)O(tw) algorithm,
this time for the MIN-CHARITY EFX ORIENTATION prob-
lem; once again, our algorithm works for multigraphs and
non-symmetric valuations.
Since this almost matches the
aforementioned lower bound (up to a log-factor), we make
progress towards settling the open question of [Deligkas et
al., 2025] on the complexity of EFX ORIENTATION for in-
stances with polynomially-bounded weights and small vertex
cover, and paint an almost complete picture on the parame-
terized complexity of EFX ORIENTATION parameterized by
treewidth, arguably the most well-studied structural parame-
ter.

Preliminaries
Throughout the paper we use standard graph notation [Di-
estel, 2017] and we assume familiarity with the basic no-
tions of parameterized complexity (cf. Appendix A). For a
graph G and S ⊆V (G) a subset of its vertices, G[S] denotes
the subgraph induced by S while G −S denotes G[V \ S].
For x, y ∈Z, let [x, y] = {z ∈Z | x ≤z ≤y} while
[x] = [1, x]. Proofs of statements marked with (⋆) are de-
ferred to the appendix.
Graphs in the context of this paper are multigraphs, i.e.,
parallel edges between two vertices may exist, unless we ex-
plicitly state that a graph is simple. All graphs considered
are undirected and without loops. We denote the set of edges
between vertices u, v of a multigraph as Euv, and the set of
edges incident to u as Eu.
2.1
Model and Problem Definitions
We are given a set of agents V = {1, . . . , n} and a set of
items E with |E| = m. Each agent i ∈[n] has a valuation
function vi : 2E →N. An allocation is a partition of E into
bundles X1, . . . , Xn, where agent i receives the bundle Xi.
A partial allocation is an allocation of some subset of E.
In the context of this work, we only consider additive valu-
ation functions, as is standard for most of the fair orientation
literature: each vi is defined over E, and can be extended to
2E with vi(X) := P
e∈X vi(e), ∀X ⊆E. As is standard, we
assume vi(∅) = 0 for all i ∈V .
Definition 1 (Envy). Given an allocation X1, . . . , Xn, we
say that agent i envies j if vi(Xi) < vi(Xj). We say that i
strongly envies j if vi(Xi) < vi(Xj \ e) for some e ∈Xj.
Definition 2 (Fair allocations). We say that an allocation is
envy-free (EF) if no agent envies another agent. We say that
an allocation is envy-free up to any good (EFX) if no agent
strongly envies another agent.
In this paper, we consider the model introduced by
[Christodoulou et al., 2023]: we are given sets of agents V
and items E that form a multigraph G = (V, E), where
for all edges e ∈Eij (i, j ∈V ) it holds that vu(e) = 0,
∀u ∈V \ {i, j}.2 Throughout the paper we may call the
agents vertices and the goods edges. We denote n = |V | and
m = |E|.
An orientation is an allocation X1, . . . , Xn, where for all
e ∈Eij (i, j ∈V ) it holds that e ∈Xi or e ∈Xj. A partial
orientation is an orientation of some subset of E. Given an
orientation, we say that e ∈Eij (i, j ∈V ) is oriented to-
wards i if e ∈Xi, i.e., if i receives e. An EF(X) orientation
is an EF(X) allocation that is an orientation.
We now define the problems which we study in this work.
EF ORIENTATION
Input: A multigraph G = (V, E) and values vi(e), vj(e)
for each e ∈Eij (i, j ∈V ).
Task: Determine whether G admits an EF orientation.
2For simplicity, we use the notation vu(e) instead of vu({e}) to
denote the valuation of a single edge e for vertex u.

MIN-CHARITY EF ORIENTATION
Input: A multigraph G = (V, E) and values vi(e), vj(e)
for each e ∈Eij (i, j ∈V ).
Task: Find the smallest integer k ≥0 s.t. G admits a
partial EF orientation of size |E| −k.
Intuitively, the MIN-CHARITY EF ORIENTATION problem
asks for the minimum amount of edges that must be removed
from a graph for an EF orientation to exist. Note that any al-
gorithm for MIN-CHARITY EF ORIENTATION also answers
EF ORIENTATION, hence, throughout the paper we may omit
an EF ORIENTATION algorithm and directly state the (more
general) MIN-CHARITY EF ORIENTATION algorithm.
The EFX ORIENTATION and MIN-CHARITY EFX ORI-
ENTATION problems are defined similarly.
We call an instance of any of these problems
• simple, if for all i, j ∈V : |Eij| ≤1,
• symmetric, if for all i, j ∈V and all e ∈Eij: vi(e) =
vj(e). In that case, it suffices to store one valuation func-
tion v: E →N, where for all i, j ∈V and all e ∈Eij:
v(e) = vi(e) = vj(e).
Throughout the paper, we may refer to the valuation func-
tions in the context of graphs as weights: each edge is rep-
resented by two weights in the general case (one for each of
its endpoints), however one weight per edge suffices if the
instance is symmetric.
3
EF Orientations
In this section we present our results concerning EF orienta-
tions. The following observation will be useful throughout
the section.
Observation 3. Edges with zero value for both endpoints can
be removed from the input without affecting the existence of
EF orientations. Similarly, edges with zero value for one end-
point and non-zero value for the other can always be oriented
towards the latter without affecting the existence of an EF ori-
entation.3

Hardness Results
Here we present various hardness results concerning the EF
ORIENTATION problem.
As our first result, we present a
lower bound based on the Strong Exponential Time Hy-
pothesis (SETH), which states that SAT does not admit any
(2 −ε)nnO(1) algorithm, where n is the number of variables.
In particular, this shows the optimality (up to polynomial fac-
tors) of the algorithm presented in Theorem 13.

3.3
Parameterization by Number of Heavy Edges
Here we consider EF ORIENTATION on simple graphs. Ob-
serve that most of the hardness results of Section 3.2 already
apply even under this restriction. Our main result is an algo-
rithm with running time 2knO(1), where k denotes the num-
ber of edges that have value at least 2 for at least one endpoint;
by Theorem 9, this is optimal (up to polynomial factors) un-
der the SETH. We remark that our algorithm works even for
binary-encoded weights on the edges.
The rough idea of our algorithm is the following. As a
first step we fix an orientation of all “heavy” edges, that is,
edges of value at least 2 for some endpoint. This leaves a
residual instance in which every unoriented edge has value 1
for both endpoints.
To appropriately orient these remain-
ing edges, we reduce to an equivalent instance of UPPER
DEGREE-CONSTRAINED GRAPH ORIENTATION, for which
a polynomial-time algorithm is known [Frank and Gy´arf´as,
1978]. This allows us, for each fixed orientation of the heavy
edges, to decide in polynomial time whether it can be ex-
tended to an envy-free orientation. Finally, iterating over all
possible orientations of the heavy edges (of which there are
at most 2k) suffices to determine whether the instance admits
an envy-free orientation.
Theorem 13 (⋆). There is an algorithm for EF ORIENTA-
TION on simple graphs running in time 2knO(1), where k is
the number of edges with value at least 2 for at least one end-
point.

3.4
Parameterization by Treewidth plus Maximum
Shared Weight
At this point, we have shown that EF ORIENTATION re-
mains computationally hard even when restricted to simple
graphs of small vertex cover and symmetric valuations: Theo-
rem 11 shows weak NP-hardness for graphs of vertex cover 2,
while for polynomially-bounded weights Theorem 12 gives a
no(vc/ log vc) lower bound under the ETH. A natural question
would be to consider whether we can match the latter lower
bound with an algorithm. We answer this positively: using
standard DP techniques, one can get an algorithm for EF
ORIENTATION running in time W O(tw)nO(1), where W de-
notes the maximum weight of the graph and tw its treewidth.
For polynomially-bounded weights, this algorithm matches
up to a log-factor the aforementioned lower bound (see The-
orem 16).
Pushing this idea further, we observe that in fact similar
techniques apply even for multigraphs with non-symmetric
valuations, under the caveat that in this setting W is a bound
on the sum of the weights of a set of parallel edges for one
of its endpoints; observe that this is a straightforward gener-
alization of the maximum weight considered in the previous
setting. We define this to be the maximum shared weight of an
instance. As a matter of fact, our main result in this section is
to show that we can solve the (more general) MIN-CHARITY
EF ORIENTATION problem in essentially the same running
time.
Theorem 14 (⋆). Given an instance I of MIN-CHARITY
EF ORIENTATION along with a nice tree decomposition of
the input multigraph of width tw, one can decide I in time
W O(tw)(n + m)O(1), where W denotes the maximum shared
weight of I and n the number of vertices.
Proof sketch. Our algorithm is a standard DP over a nice tree
decomposition of the input graph. For a node t of the given
nice tree decomposition, let Gt denote the corresponding sub-
graph, while Bt denotes the set of vertices appearing in the

EFX Orientations
The following observations will be useful throughout this sec-
tion. Recall the definition of strong envy from Theorem 1.
Observation 17. An agent that has only received one good
is not strongly envied by any agent.
Observation 18. For agents i, j, if i does not strongly envy j
and j has received a good g s.t. vi(g) = 0, then i does not
envy j.
4.1
From EF to EFX Orientations
It was first proven in [Christodoulou et al., 2023] that EFX
ORIENTATION is NP-complete even with symmetric valua-
tions; this hardness persists even for simple graphs of ver-
tex cover number 8 or for multigraphs consisting of 10 ver-
tices, where the problem was proven to be weakly NP-
complete [Deligkas et al., 2025]. In a follow-up work, [Afsh-
inmehr et al., 2025] show NP-completeness even for symmet-
ric instances on bipartite multigraphs with 8 vertices. Here we
present a reduction from EF ORIENTATION to EFX ORIEN-
TATION that, along with Section 3.2, allows us to complement
and improve upon the aforementioned results. See Figure 4
for an overview of our reduction.

Theorem 19 (⋆). There is a polynomial-time, logarithmic-

valuation. The reduction preserves simplicity.

Combining Theorem 19 with the hardness results in Sec-
tion 3.2 leads to Theorems 20 and 21.
Corollary 20. EFX ORIENTATION is weakly NP-complete
even restricted to
• simple symmetric instances of vertex cover number 4,
• symmetric instances with 4 vertices.
Corollary 21. EFX ORIENTATION is W[1]-hard parameter-
ized by vertex cover number, XNLP-hard parameterized by
pathwidth, and XALP-hard parameterized by treewidth, even
restricted to simple symmetric instances with polynomially-
bounded weights.
Furthermore, for such instances, EFX
ORIENTATION does not admit a no(vc/ log vc) algorithm un-
der the ETH, where vc denotes the vertex cover number of
the input graph.
We now compare Theorems 20 and 21 with existing lit-
erature. Theorem 20 improves upon the constants 8 and 10
from [Deligkas et al., 2025] that we mention at the start of the

results for instances with polynomially-bounded weights and
small vertex cover, partially answering an open question of
[Deligkas et al., 2025] regarding the hardness of EFX ORI-
ENTATION in this setting. In the following subsection, we
complement the lower bound from Theorem 21 with an algo-
rithm.
4.2

allows binary-encoded weights, then the problem remains
NP-hard even for simple graphs of constant vertex cover, or
multigraphs of constant number of vertices. Consequently,
in the following we consider the case where all weights
are polynomially-bounded; this is exactly the regime for
which [Deligkas et al., 2025] have explicitly asked whether
the structure of the input graph may prove algorithmically

5
Conclusion
In this work we initiated the study of EF orientations and pre-
sented a variety of both positive and negative results regard-
ing their computational complexity. We additionally studied
them under the perspective of parameterized complexity, and
showed how some of the results can even be transferred to the
related EFX ORIENTATION problem, improving upon previ-
ous work on the latter in the process.
As a direction for future work, we comment that Theo-
rem 13 generalizes Theorem 6, albeit only for simple graphs.
A natural question is thus whether we can develop such an
algorithm that works for multigraphs as well, that is, whether
EF ORIENTATION admits an FPT algorithm parameterized
by the number k of heavy edges running in time 2knO(1).
Another interesting direction would be to ask for regimes
for which the complexity of EF and EFX ORIENTATION dif-
fers. We showed that one such case regards instances with

binary valuations, while the two problems have similar be-
havior in case of polynomially-bounded weights and small
vertex cover/treewidth. In which other (valuation or struc-
tural) regime does the complexity of the two problems differ?

Parameterized Complexity
In this section we briefly review some basic notions of parameterized complexity which we use throughout the paper; for a
more thorough exposition we refer to standard textbooks, e.g., [Cygan et al., 2015; Downey and Fellows, 2013].
An instance of a parameterized problem is a pair (x, k), where x is the main input (with size n := |x|) and k ∈N is the
parameter. We say that a parameterized problem is fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) and belongs to the class FPT if it admits an
algorithm of running time f(k) · nO(1) for some computable function f.
Let Π1 and Π2 be two parameterized problems. A parameterized reduction (also known as an FPT reduction) from Π1 to
Π2 is an algorithm that, given an instance (x, k) of Π1, produces an instance (x′, k′) of Π2 such that:
• (x, k) is a yes-instance of Π1 if and only if (x′, k′) is a yes-instance of Π2,
• k′ ≤g(k) for some computable function g,
• the running time of the algorithm is f(k) · |x|O(1) for some computable function f.
Notice that if Π2 is fixed-parameter tractable (FPT), then so is Π1.
We will use parameterized reductions to show hardness results for parameterized problems. In particular, we define the class
W[1] as the set of parameterized problems that are FPT-reducible to k-CLIQUE parameterized by k.5 It holds that FPT ⊆W[1],
and it is widely believed that FPT ̸= W[1], thus showing that a problem is W[1]-hard is strong evidence against fixed-parameter
tractability [Cygan et al., 2015; Downey and Fellows, 2013].
Another complexity class considered in this paper is XNLP. This class was recently introduced by [Bodlaender et al., 2024],
and it consists of parameterized problems decidable by a nondeterministic algorithm that, on inputs of size n with parameter
k, uses time f(k) · nO(1) and space f(k) · log n, for some computable function f. It holds that W[1] ⊆XNLP, and in fact
XNLP-hardness implies W[t]-hardness for all t ∈N. In order to show XNLP-hardness results, we need to introduce the notion
of parameterized logspace reductions, which are simply parameterized reductions that use O(g(k)+log |x|) space, where g is a
computable function. In that case, if Π1 is XNLP-hard and there is a parameterized logspace reduction from Π1 to Π2, then Π2
is also XNLP-hard [Bodlaender et al., 2024]. Another class we use is XALP, which can be seen as the “tree variant” of XNLP,
where we additionally allow access to an auxiliary stack (with only top element lookup allowed) [Bodlaender et al., 2022c].
This class contains XNLP, and XALP-hardness can be shown via parameterized logspace reductions as well. XNLP and XALP
have been shown to be the “natural home” of many problems parameterized by pathwidth and treewidth respectively, as many
problems tend to be complete for each class respectively.
The treewidth of a graph is a measure of how close the graph is to being a tree, and is arguably the most well-studied
structural parameter. A tree-decomposition T of a graph G is a pair (T, {Bt | t ∈V (T)}) where T is a tree and each node
t of T is assigned a bag Bt ⊆V (G), such that: (i) every vertex v ∈V (G) is contained in some bag Bt; (ii) for every edge
{u, v} ∈E(G) there exists t ∈V (T) with {u, v} ⊆Bt; and (iii) for every v ∈V (G), the set {t ∈V (T) | v ∈Bt}
induces a connected subtree of T. The width is maxt∈V (T ) |Bt| −1; the treewidth tw(G) is the minimum width over all tree-
decompositions of G. A tree-decomposition is nice if each node of T has one of the following types: (i) Leaf nodes t where
Bt = ∅; (ii) Introduce nodes t with a unique child t′ such that Bt = Bt′ ∪{v} for some v ∈V (G); (iii) Forget nodes t with
a unique child t′ such that Bt′ = Bt ∪{v} for some v ∈V (G); (iv) Join nodes t with exactly two children t1, t2 such that
Bt = Bt1 = Bt2. It is known that given any tree decomposition one can in linear time construct a nice tree decomposition of the
same width. Computing a tree-decomposition of minimum width is in FPT when parameterized by the treewidth [Bodlaender,
1996; Kloks, 1994; Korhonen and Lokshtanov, 2023], and even more efficient algorithms exist for obtaining near-optimal
tree-decompositions [Korhonen, 2021].
We conclude this section by recalling two well-known hypotheses from the area of fine-grained complexity [Impagliazzo
and Paturi, 2001; Impagliazzo et al., 2001]. The Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) posits that there exists a constant c > 0
such that no algorithm solves 3-SAT on n variables and m clauses in time 2c(n+m) · (n + m)O(1). The Strong Exponential
Time Hypothesis (SETH) states that for every ε > 0 there exists a constant k ≥3 (depending only on ε) such that no algorithm
solves k-SAT on n variables in time (2 −ε)n · nO(1). Both hypotheses constitute standard tools to obtain oftentimes tight
(conditional) lower bounds. With respect to parameterized complexity, they have been used to pinpoint the best parametric
dependence possible for various parameterized algorithms [Lokshtanov et al., 2018a; Lokshtanov et al., 2018b].

We now prove the converse: if I′ has an EFX orientation, then I has an EF orientation. Take an EFX orientation O′ of I′.
Without loss of generality, assume e∗is oriented towards d1 in O′. Then, for O′ to be an EFX orientation, d1 must not receive
any other edge (otherwise d2 would strongly envy d1), implying that all other edges incident to d1 are oriented away from d1
in O′. Hence, every vertex v ∈V has received a zero-weight edge in O′. For all u, v ∈V , u does not strongly envy v because
O′ is EFX; by Theorem 18, this implies that u does not envy v. Hence, orienting all edges in E in accordance to O′ results in
an EF orientation O of I.
The reduction described uses logarithmic space,10 since only two vertices and O(n) edges of polynomial weight are added
to the graph.
