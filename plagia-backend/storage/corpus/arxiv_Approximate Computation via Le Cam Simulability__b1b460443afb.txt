Approximate Computation via Le Cam Simulability
Deniz Akdemir
December 2025
Abstract
We propose a decision-theoretic framework for computational complexity,
complementary to classical theory: moving from syntactic exactness (Turing
/ Shannon) to semantic simulability (Le Cam). While classical theory classi-
fies problems by the cost of exact solution, modern computation often seeks only
decision-valid approximations. We introduce a framework where "computation" is
viewed as the efficient simulation of a target statistical experiment within a bounded
risk distortion (Le Cam deficiency).
We formally define computational deficiency (δpoly) and use it to construct
the complexity class LeCam-P (Decision-Robust Polynomial Time), characterizing
problems that may be syntactically hard but semantically easy to approximate. We
show that classical Karp reductions can be viewed as zero-deficiency simulations,
and that approximate reductions correspond to bounded deficiency. Furthermore,
we establish the No-Free-Transfer Inequality, showing that strictly invariant
representations inevitably destroy decision-relevant information. This framework
offers a statistical perspective on approximation theory, bridging the gap between
algorithmic complexity and decision theory.
Keywords: Le Cam deficiency, computational complexity, approximate reduc-
tion, semantic simulability, statistical experiments

Introduction: The Crisis of Exactness
"The quantity of information... is a measure of the statistical properties of
the message source. It is not, and implies no reference to, the meaning of the
message." — Claude Shannon (1948)
1.1
The Problem: Computation as Decision
For seventy years, the foundations of Theoretical Computer Science [Turing, 1936] and
Information Theory [Shannon, 1948] have been built on syntax. To Turing, a computa-
tion is a sequence of symbol manipulations where any deviation is an error. To Shannon,
reliable communication requires the exact reproduction of bits.
This exactness paradigm was necessary for the era of reliable transmission and log-
ical calculation. However, it can be overly restrictive for high-dimensional statistics and
machine learning. In these domains, we rarely require the exact answer to a combinatorial
problem; we require an answer that leads to optimal decisions.
Consider the Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP). Syntactically, finding the
exact minimal tour is NP-hard. Semantically, however, a heuristic that produces a tour
1
arXiv:2512.24860v1 [math.ST] 31 Dec 2025

within 0.1% of the optimum for 99.9% of instances is "effectively" optimal for almost all
downstream decision tasks (e.g., logistics planning). Standard complexity theory treats
the heuristic as a failure (it does not solve TSP). We lack a rigorous language to obtain
credit for "functionally equivalent" but numerically distinct computations.
We address a Gap in Exactness: We have precise measures for the cost of computa-
tion (time/space), but need a more rigorous measure for the semantic value of the result
in decision-making contexts.
1.2
The Proposed Paradigm: Computation as Simulation
We propose a decision-centric lens for computational theory: viewing computation as
experiment simulation.
We posit that the fundamental primitive in this context is not just the function f(x),
but the Statistical Experiment E [Le Cam, 1964]. An experiment is a family of prob-
ability measures representing the link between data and the unknown state of nature. In
this framework, an algorithm can be viewed as a Markov Kernel attempting to simulate
a target experiment.
The central question scales from:
• Classical: "Can we compute f(x) exactly in polynomial time?"
• Proposed: "Can we simulate the decision-making capabilities of the ideal ex-
periment E using a polynomial-time kernel, such that for any bounded decision
problem, the risk inflation is at most ϵ?"
This is the domain of Le Cam Deficiency. We introduce Computational Defi-
ciency (δpoly), a complexity measure that quantifies the minimum error of an efficient
simulator. This allows us to define "Semantic Complexity": a problem is easy not because
we can solve it exactly, but because we can simulate it efficiently.

Experiments, Deficiency, and Computation
This section establishes the rigorous mathematical machinery that unifies representation,
computation, and information. We begin with the statistical foundations—Experiments
and Deficiency—and then introduce Computational Deficiency, the central object
of this manuscript, which bridges the gap between information-theoretic existence and
algorithmic feasibility.
2.1
Statistical Experiments and Decision Problems
Definition 2.1 (Statistical Experiment). A statistical experiment is a triple E =
(X, Θ, {Pθ}θ∈Θ) where:
• X is a measurable sample space (observations/instance data),

The Computational Gap
Proposition 2.1 (Hierarchy). Always δ(E, F) ≤δpoly(E, F).
The Separation Question: Do there exist experiments where δ ≈0 but δpoly ≈1?
• Case δ ≈0: The information is present in the data (statistically sufficient).

• Equivalence requires simulation in both directions.

Risk Interpretation
Theorem 2.1 (Computational Risk Transfer).
If δpoly(E, F) ≤ϵ, then for any
decision problem solvable in F with risk r, there exists a poly-time algorithm in E
achieving risk r + ϵ.
This theorem justifies using approximations: if the computational deficiency is low,
the approximate algorithm is guaranteed to be decision-equivalent to the exact one.

4
Approximate Computation as Simulation
The P versus NP problem asks whether every problem whose solution can be quickly
verified can also be quickly solved. The Le Cam version asks: can it be quickly simulated
for the purpose of optimal decision making?
Classical complexity theory classifies problems based on the cost of exact solution
(computing f(x) precisely). However, in modern AI and large-scale science, we rarely care
about exactness (which is often impossible due to noise or model misspecification). We
care about decision validity: can we output an answer that typically leads to optimal
decisions?
In this section, we extend Le Cam’s theory from statistics to computation, defining
the class LeCam-P and the notion of Decision-Robust Computation. This explains
why "NP-hard" problems (like protein folding or TSP) are empirically solvable by neural
heuristics: they are syntactically hard, but semantically easy.
4.1
The Computational Experiment
To bridge text-book algorithms with statistical experiments, we map discrete problems
to generating processes.
Definition 4.1 (Computational Experiment). For a computational problem Π
(e.g., SAT, TSP) with instances x ∈X and solutions y ∈Y, we view each instance as
defining a Single-Instance Experiment EΠ:
• Parameter Space Θ = X (the instances).
• Sample Space: The set of valid solutions (or certificates).
• Distributions: Px = δy∗(x) (a point mass on the optimal solution).
Note: In this setting, the parameters are the instances themselves. While this creates
a degenerate statistical structure (no sampling noise), the "noise" arising in simulation
is computational.
4.2
Le Cam Complexity Classes
We introduce a complexity class defined not by worst-case runtime alone, but by the
trade-off between runtime and deficiency.
9

Definition 4.2 (Decision-Robust Polynomial Time: LeCam-P). A problem Π
is in LeCam-P if for every polynomially evaluable bounded decision problem (A, ℓ),
there exists a randomized algorithm Aapprox such that:
1. Efficiency: Aapprox runs in time polynomial in the instance size |x|.
2. Simulability: The experiment induced by the algorithm, Ealg = {P alg
x }, satisfies:
δpoly(EΠ, Ealg) ≤ϵ
for arbitrarily small ϵ.
Interpretation: A problem is in LeCam-P if we can simulate the "God View"
(exact solution) sufficiently well to make optimal decisions on efficiently verifiable tasks.
4.2.1
Syntactic Hardness vs. Semantic Ease
This framework formalizes the distinction between syntactic and semantic hardness:
• Syntactically Hard: TSP /∈P. Finding the exact tour is hard.
• Semantically Easy: TSP ∈LeCam-P (hypothetically).
Finding a tour that
preserves decision utility (e.g., logistic cost ≤threshold) is easy.
Neural networks are Low-Deficiency Simulators of hard functions. They do not
find the exact minimum (high syntactic error), but they reproduce the decision statistics
of the optimum (low semantic error).

Impossibility & Asymmetry
"Freedom is not worth having if it does not include the freedom to make
mistakes." — Mahatma Gandhi
We have established that valid representations are those that allow us to simulate
the target experiment. In this section, we contrast this with the popular "Invariance"
paradigm. We derive the No-Free-Transfer Principle, which proves that strictly in-
variant representations inevitably destroy decision-relevant information when the source
and target domains are structurally distinct. This result serves as a canonical example
of how syntactic constraints can conflict with semantic simulability.
5.1
The Invariance Problem
A common approach to transfer learning (e.g., Domain-Adversarial Neural Networks
[Ganin et al., 2016]) is to enforce marginal invariance:
PS(Z) ≈PT(Z)
where Z = T(X) is the representation. The intuition is to remove "domain-specific"
information.
However, this requirement ignores the structure of the experiment (the conditional
link P(X|θ)). If the Source and Target experiments differ in intrinsic difficulty (e.g.,
Signal-to-Noise Ratio), enforcing marginal invariance forces the representation to degrade
the higher-quality source to match the lower-quality target.
5.1.1
Example: The Gaussian Collapse
Consider two experiments:
• Source: ES = {N(θ, 1)}θ∈R

• Target: ET = {N(θ, σ2)}θ∈R with σ > 1.

"It is not enough to know, one must also apply; it is not enough to will, one

Discussion & Open Problems
In this manuscript, we have argued that the classical foundations of computation and
information—based on syntactic exactness—are insufficient for the era of approximate,
decision-centric AI. We proposed a shift to Le Cam Simulability, where validity is
determined not by bitwise reproduction but by the preservation of decision utility.
7.1
Summary: Approximate Computation via Simulation
We unified three distinct fields under the banner of Experiment Simulation:
1. Statistics: Representation learning is the search for a sufficient experiment.
2. Information Theory: Channel capacity is the maximin rate of deficient simula-
tion.
3. Complexity: Hard problems are those where the "God View" experiment is com-
putationally non-simulable (δpoly ≈1).
Our central technical contribution is the Hierarchy of Representation Equiv-
alence and the LeCam-P class, which provide the vocabulary to distinguish between
errors that matter and errors that effectively vanish under decision-theoretic aggregation.
7.2
Limitations
Our framework is foundational, yet its current form relies on assumptions that invite
further scrutiny:
14

1. Asymptotic Normality: The consistency results rely on Local Asymptotic Nor-
mality (LAN). Extending this to non-regular, heavy-tailed, or singular models (com-
mon in deep learning) remains a challenge.
2. Computational Overhead: While we proved existence, finding the optimal sim-
ulator via MMD optimization is computationally expensive (O(N2)).
Efficient
approximations (e.g., Random Fourier Features [Rahimi and Recht, 2007]) trade
deficiency for speed, a tradeoff that requires tighter bounds.
7.3
Broader Implications
While abstract, this theory has immediate consequences for applied domains.
• AI Regulation: The ability to certify specific decision classes allows for "Partial
Approval" of AI systems (e.g., approved for screening but not diagnosis), replacing
binary "safe/unsafe" labels with nuanced deficiency bounds.
• Financial Risk: The distinction between invariance and simulability explains the
failure of "robust" models during market regime shifts, suggesting that directional
simulation is the only rigorous path to risk parity.
7.4
Open Problems
The shift to semantic computation opens vast new horizons.
7.4.1
The "Le Cam Limit" of Computation
Landauer’s principle [Landauer, 1961] relates information erasure to energy. We conjec-
ture a thermodynamic bound on semantic processing: reducing the deficiency of a
simulation by δ requires a minimum energy cost proportional to log(1/δ). This would
define the "Le Cam Limit" of a physical computer—the maximum rate at which it can
extract decision-relevant information.
7.4.2
Finite-Sample Concentration
Our bounds are asymptotic. A critical open problem is deriving non-asymptotic concen-
tration inequalities for deficiency in deep neural networks. Can we bound P(|ˆδ −δ| > ϵ)
in terms of the network’s VC-dimension or Rademacher complexity?
7.4.3
Quantum Le Cam Theory
Can we extend deficiency to quantum channels (CPTP maps)? A "quantum deficiency"
metric could unify quantum hypothesis testing and channel capacities, potentially offering
a decision-theoretic lens on the quantum measurement problem itself.

Counterexamples for Strictness
We now exhibit explicit counterexamples showing that each inclusion is strict.
Counterexample 1: Likelihood Distortion ̸⊆Sufficiency.
Consider the Gaussian location family E = {N(θ, 1) : θ ∈R} on X = R. Define the
representation T(x) = ⌊x⌋(floor function, i.e., binning into unit intervals).
• Sufficiency fails: The conditional distribution Pθ(X|T(X) = k) is N(θ, 1) truncated
to [k, k + 1). This distribution depends on θ (the truncation bounds shift relative
to the mode). Hence T is not sufficient.
• Bounded Likelihood Distortion holds: For z = k, the induced measure Qθ satisfies
Qθ(k) = Φ(k+1−θ)−Φ(k−θ). For any θ, θ′ with |θ−θ′| ≤1, the ratio Qθ(k)/Qθ′(k)
is bounded (no bin has probability zero for either). Numerical computation shows
the log-likelihood ratio distortion is bounded by ϵ ≈2 for |θ −θ′| ≤1.
Thus, T achieves bounded likelihood distortion but is not sufficient.
