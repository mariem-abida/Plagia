ABSTRACT
Strategic dialogue requires agents to execute distinct dialogue acts,
for which belief estimation is essential. While prior work often
estimates beliefs accurately, it lacks a principled mechanism to
use those beliefs during generation. We bridge this gap by first
formalizing two core acts Adversarial and Alignment—and by
operationalizing them via probabilistic constraints on what an
agent may generate. We instantiate this idea in BEDA, a frame-
work that consists of the world set, the belief estimator for belief
estimation, and the conditional generator that selects acts and re-
alizes utterances consistent with the inferred beliefs. Across three
settings, Conditional Keeper–Burglar (adversarial), Mutual Friends
(cooperative), and CaSiNo (negotiation), BEDA consistently out-
performs strong baselines: on CKBG it improves success rate by at
least 5.0 points across backbones and by 20.6 points with GPT-
4.1-nano; on Mutual Friends it achieves an average improvement
of 9.3 points; and on CaSiNo it achieves the optimal deal relative
to all baselines. These results indicate that casting belief estimation
as constraints provides a simple, general mechanism for reliable
strategic dialogue.

INTRODUCTION
Complex dialogue settings such as negotiation [13, 24], debate [39],
deception for good [15], and common-ground alignment [33] are
ubiquitous for AI agents. In these scenarios, an agent must strate-
gically perform dialogue acts to shape the interlocutor’s beliefs to
achieve its own objectives [9, 14, 25]. For example, in negotiation,
the agent should align and emphasize common preferences to
increase the chance its proposal is accepted, while simultaneously

Executing such strategic acts depends on two essential compo-
nents [9, 50]: (i) accurate belief estimation and (ii) effective
use of those beliefs for utterance generation. While prior
works [18, 33, 54] have demonstrated the benefits of modeling these
subprocesses explicitly, they have only emphasized the former, leav-
ing the latter underdeveloped. In particular, existing methods often
relay all available belief information without a principled criterion
for what to reveal and how to reveal it–an approach that can be
suboptimal when belief states are complex. We therefore bridge
belief estimation and utterance generation with a mechanism that se-
lectively and purposefully transforms beliefs into dialogue actions.
We begin by giving precise definitions of Alignment and Ad-
versarial dialogue acts within the game-theoretic framework of
beliefs and common knowledge [2, 5, 6], thereby bridging belief
estimation and dialogue behavior. Conceptually, an Adversarial
Dialogue Act , introduces or emphasizes events that lie outside
the opponent’s current belief support, strategically steering inter-
action toward some aggressive goals. By contrast, an Alignment
Dialogue Act , restricts utterances to events entailed by shared
common knowledge, promoting mutual understanding and coordi-
nation. In this view, belief estimation over events functions as
constraints that define the permissible space of dialogue acts and
operationalize their selection during generation.
The proposed agent framework, BEDA (Belief Estimation for
Dialogue Acts), shown in Figure 1, formalizes these conceptual
definitions to support empirical experimentation and validate the
underlying theoretical constructs. At a high level, BEDA consists of
three core components: World Set, Belief Estimator Module, and Con-
ditional Generator. The World Set serves as a structured representa-
tion of the environment, organizing events to provide a foundation
for belief formation. The Belief Estimator Module utilizes this struc-
tured information to infer the opponent’s perspective of the world,
thereby modeling their beliefs. Finally, the Conditional Generator
synthesizes the estimated belief states to generate utterances that
align with the defined criteria for alignment and adversarial actions,

We further evaluate the effectiveness of our approach on three
distinct datasets: Conditional Keeper-Burglar Game (CKBG), corre-

personalized misleading information in adversarial settings.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
• We mathematically define the concepts of Adversarial Dia-
logue Act and Alignment Dialogue Act based on a rigorous
formulation of belief estimation and incorporate them into our
framework, BEDA . This integration bridges the gap between
belief estimation and dialogue act generation.
• For evaluation, we refine the Keeper–Burglar dataset intro-
duced by Hagendorff [15]. Our results underscore the impor-
tance of accurate belief estimation and show that the ef-
fectiveness of estimation functions as constraints on both
Adversarial Dialogue Act and Alignment Dialogue Act behaviors.
• This integration of belief estimation in BEDA enhances the per-
formance of our framework, yielding substantial improvements
when compared to baseline models and other existing methods.

2.3
Components of the BEDA framework
In this subsection, we’ll describe our framework, named BEDA ,
depicted mathematically in Section 2.2. As illustrated in Figure 1,
BEDA is composed of three components: the world set, the belief
estimator module, and the conditional generator.
World Set P(𝑊). The world set represents a finite collection of
events. Each element of this set corresponds to an event that has
been referenced in the dialogue or is relevant to the environment.
Events within the world set can only be either true or false. Further-
more, not all events are shared as common knowledge between the
two agents. Consequently, events specified in the set require addi-
tional discrimination and prediction. In this work, since we focus
mainly on the whole paradigm, the world set is given, eliminating
the need for dynamic construction, leaving only the attributes to
be predicted.
Belief Estimator (𝑃𝐴). The belief estimator module comprises two
belief estimators: one dedicated to determining the truthfulness of
events i.e. [𝑃𝐴(𝐸|𝐶), ∀𝐸∈P(𝑊)] and the other functioning as a
predictor of the interlocutor’s beliefs i.e. [𝑃𝐴(𝐾𝐵𝐸|𝐶), ∀𝐸∈P(𝑊)].
In this framework, each belief corresponds to an event from world
set. The belief estimator module generates beliefs by analyzing the
content of the dialogue and aligning it with the world set. How-
ever, it is not feasible for the module to store all possible settings.
Consequently, a robust generalization capability is essential for
effective belief estimation. Therefore, we employ a pre-trained en-
coder model, the BERT model [10], as the backbone. Given the
input 𝐶, the model then outputs the vectors [𝑃𝐴(𝐸|𝐶), ∀𝐸∈P(𝑊)]

and [𝑃𝐴(𝐾𝐵𝐸|𝐶), ∀𝐸∈P(𝑊)]. Each entry is the model’s estimate
of the truth probability of 𝐸and, respectively, of 𝐾𝐵𝐸.
Conditional Generator (𝜋). The final component is the conditional
generator, which synthesizes the context 𝐶, encompassing both the
dialogue history and the task, alongside the event 𝐸. The event
𝐸is selected according to the predictions of the belief estimator
module and the chosen dialogue act. For instance, in the case of
Adversarial Dialogue Act , only 𝐸satisfying both 𝑃𝐴(𝐸) ≥1 −
𝜖and 𝑃𝐴(¬𝐾𝐵𝐸) ≥1 −𝜖are picked. The conditional generator
then utilizes this integrated information to generate response 𝑢
to the interlocutor, represented as 𝜋(𝑢|𝐸,𝐶). We hypothesize that
incorporating enhanced beliefs into the generator significantly
improves its performance and response quality.
2.4
Learning and Inference of BEDA
To mitigate the substantial computational cost associated with fine-
tuning large language models, we opt to fix the LLM in the con-
ditional generation module (𝜋(𝑢|𝐸,𝐶)) and leave solely the belief
estimator as a trainable part. The training of the belief estimator
is performed under a supervised setting, where a set of dialogue
data with known belief ground truth is collected and utilized for
learning. To avoid data leakage, separate data are used for training
and inference. We find that supervised training belief estimators
can be generalized to unseen settings well. The inference process
of BEDA is illustrated in Figure 1. In this framework, the context,
background information, and the world set are provided to two
belief estimators, which predict the corresponding beliefs. These
predicted beliefs, along with the basic input information, are then
fed into the conditional generator to produce an utterance. This
generated utterance is subsequently presented to the interlocutor
model to elicit a response.

DATASETS
We conduct three experiments using three datasets: a competitive
experiment based on a refined dataset from the Keeper-Burglar
Game [15], a cooperative experiment with mutual friends [16], and a
mixed experiment utilizing the CaSiNo dataset [8]. The first experi-
ment tests the Adversarial Dialogue Act, while the second examines
the Alignment Dialogue Act. The third experiment provides a com-
parative framework to analyze both fundamental dialogue acts. In
all experiments, the configuration follows a dialogic interaction
paradigm between two LLM agents. The proposed framework is im-
plemented on one agent in the Keeper-Burglar Game [15], as shown
in Figure 1, and applied to both agents in the other experiments.
Conditional Keeper-Burglar Game (CKBG). Building upon the
Keeper-Burglar game [15], in which the agent, acting as the keeper,
aims to deceive the burglar into selecting an incorrect container,
we extend the original framework. This extension incorporates
additional environmental conditions to increase the diversity of
the game and to better simulate more realistic dialogue scenarios.
We refer to this enhanced framework as the novel dataset Condi-
tional Keeper-Burglar Game (CKBG). In this game, the world
set comprises the environmental conditions, and the belief estima-
tor predicts the specific conditions of which the burglar is aware.
Moreover, during the selection process, the condition known to the

Mutual Friends (MF). MF [16] is the task of identifying a mutual
friend between two agents. It’s a cooperative setting where the
Alignment Dialogue Act is suitable. The world set is designed as
various attributes possessed by the friend, and the belief estimator
predicts the values of these attributes as believed by the other player.
In the conditional generation phase, the estimation of the other’s
belief will be used as a condition to generate information within
the common ground.
CaSiNo. CaSiNo [8] is a negotiation task in which two partici-
pants assume the roles of campsite neighbors negotiating the al-
location of resources. In this context, the world set represents the
preferences of both negotiators. To capture the alignment and ad-
versarial nature of the negotiation, we introduce two distinct belief
estimators. The first estimator predicts the preferences of the op-
ponent, while the second estimates our own preferences from the
opponent’s perspective. The selection mechanism operates as fol-
lows: in the Alignment Dialogue Act , an element that is known to
both parties is selected, while in the Adversarial Dialogue Act , an
element known exclusively to our side (and not to the opponent) is
chosen. These two selected elements are subsequently combined
for the conditional generation module.

4.2
Conditional Keeper-Burglar Game (CKBG)
The results on CKBG, as presented in Table 2, provide a comparative
analysis of the performance of five distinct approaches. We compare
BEDA with the performance of four approaches: w/o belief (without
belief estimation, relying solely on the language model itself.), w/o
belief + CoT (using Chain-of-Thought (CoT) technique [42]), w/o
belief + Self Reflect (using self reflect technique [38]), random belief
(utilizing randomized belief estimation, each time the randomized
selection process is conducted.). The belief estimator is trained
following the procedure described in Section 2.4. Specifically, we
construct 400 data points from the first 50 settings of the original
dataset and employ two w/o belief GPT-3.5 models to generate
dialogue data with randomized belief annotations. To prevent data
leakage, these 50 settings are distinct from those used for testing.
A comparative analysis of the experimental results between the
BEDA and the w/o belief model reveals a consistent performance
improvement when belief estimations are employed as probabilis-
tic constraints. Specifically, the success rate exhibits a minimum
increase of 5.0 when LLaMA3.1-8b is used as the backbone model,
with the maximum improvement reaching 20.6 when Qwen2.5-14b
is utilized as the backbone. Furthermore, in comparison to widely
used methods such as CoT [42] and Self-Reflect [38], BEDA demon-
strates superior performance, surpassing these approaches by 15.5%
and 15.4%, respectively, when averaged across all backbones. These
findings underscore the effectiveness of functioning belief es-
timations as probabilistic constraints in performing Adver-
sarial Dialogue Act .
Furthermore, the precision of belief estimation is also crucial.
BEDA consistently surpasses the random belief baseline across
all backbone models by a substantial margin. On average, BEDA
achieves a success rate exceeding that of random belief by more
than 9.5 points across the evaluated models. The minimum observed
improvement is 4.8, recorded with the LLaMA3.1-8b model, while
the maximum enhancement reaches 14.0% when employing GPT-
4.1-nano as the backbone. This sustained performance superiority
across diverse models suggests that enhanced accuracy in belief
estimation significantly contributes to improved outcomes.
4.3
Mutual Friends (MF)
The results for MF are presented in Table 3. We utilize the baselines
defined in Section 4.2 and include MindDial proposed by Qiu et al.
[33] as an additional baseline. MindDial provides the LLM-agent
with all available belief information through prompts, without im-
posing constraints. The training of our belief estimator follows the
procedure described in Section 4.2, and the complete test set from
the original dataset was used for evaluation.
Compared with MindDial, BEDA shows that belief estimation
alone is insufficient; a better usage mechanism is needed. As
shown by the success rate (SR) in Table 3, BEDA attains state-of-
the-art results on GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4o-mini and Qwen2.5-7B,
delivering an average gain of 9.3 points over the w/o belief variant—
the largest among all baselines—with a maximum improvement of
30.4 when using GPT-3.5 as the backbone LLM. Although MindDial
also estimates beliefs, it does not constrain which parts of the belief
state are exploited for the task; accordingly, on GPT-4 its win rate
(76.0%) is essentially indistinguishable from using a random belief
state (75.1%), leading to suboptimal performance. These results
underscore that leveraging the estimated belief state to impose
probabilistic constraints can improve cooperative success
rate.
In addition to SR, we also calculated the ratios of SR over aver-
age turns and SR over average tokens to reflect the efficiency of
information exchange. The experimental results in Table 3 show
that the BEDA method achieves the highest average SR while re-
quiring the fewest average turns. Its SR/# Avg. Turn outperforms
the baseline method across all experimental settings, with an aver-
age improvement of 2.3 compared to w/o belief. At the same time,
BEDA ’s average SR/# Avg. Token also reaches an optimal value
of 0.11, which outperforms all comparison methods. The above
results clearly demonstrate that probabilistic constraints based
on belief estimation can also enhance the efficiency of in-
formation exchange in cooperative tasks. BEDA steers both
agents’ content toward evidence diagnostic of the mutual friend,
encourages more active elimination of incorrect candidates, and
exchanges new information. Although this richer exchange makes
individual utterances longer, the dyad completes the task in fewer

turns, achieving a higher success rate as well as lower #Avg. Turn
and #Avg. Token, which means higher cooperative efficiency.

We compare BEDA ’s results against the MindDial baseline [33],

For the closed-source model, which is built upon GPT-3.5 and

RELATED WORKS
Dialogue Acts Generation. Dialogue acts, defined as functional
units of communication in speech act theory [3], govern intentional
interactions such as persuasion, negotiation, and debate. In the
early days, people focused on dialogue act classification [17, 30, 35],
whereas nowadays with the rise of LLMs, generation is important.
Persuasion emphasizes attitude modification, where computational
models optimize strategies [45] such as integrating emotional intel-
ligence [36]. Negotiation centers on multi-party interest coordina-
tion, requiring balancing collaborative and competitive dynamics
[7, 11, 24, 48]. Debate prioritizes logical argumentation structures
[27, 41, 53]. Text-based social deduction games [28, 37, 44, 46, 47]
serve as important application scenarios for dialogue act generation.
Theory-of-Mind and Opponent Modeling. Theory of Mind (ToM)
refers to the cognitive ability to understand mental states like be-
liefs and intentions of oneself and others, and to predict behavior
according to the states [23]. Recent studies have shown that LLMs
have ToM abilities to some extent, as they are able to distinguish
between different belief states of multiple agents [22, 56]. In the con-
text of opponent modeling [29], ToM serves as a prevalent approach
for modeling agents [34, 52]. Recent advancements in opponent
modeling for Reinforcement Learning (RL) have enabled RL models
to dynamically adapt their strategies during execution by respond-
ing to opponents employing unknown and changeable algorithms
[1, 21, 51]. Furthermore, opponent modeling has practical applica-
tions in strategic game scenarios, such as poker [4], contract bridge
[26] and deception game [15].
7
DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORKS
Applying LLMs for belief estimation. While large language mod-
els (LLMs) can serve as belief estimators, our experiments indicate
that lightweight encoders suffice to infer belief states from dialogue
with substantial efficiency gains. In particular, a compact encoder
captures the salient conversational signals with accuracy compara-
ble to an LLM at a fraction of the computational cost. We therefore
employ the smaller model in practice, reserving LLM-based estima-
tors for settings that demand broader world knowledge or enhanced
robustness to distribution shift.
Dynamic construction of the world set. To isolate the contribu-
tion of belief estimation and its interaction with dialogue acts, this
work holds the world set fixed. Future work may relax this assump-
tion by learning a dynamic world set that is expanded, pruned, or
reweighted online based on interaction signals, potentially improv-
ing coverage and downstream performance.
Finer modeling of dialogue acts. Although many specific acts (e.g.,
AGREEMENT, typically subsumed under Alignment Dialogue Act
) fall within the coarse categories Adversarial Dialogue Act and
Alignment Dialogue Act , finer-grained act modeling may yield
stronger agents. Hierarchical or compositional schemas that dis-
tinguish, for example, Agreement, Concession, Hedging, and
Commitment, could provide more precise control and better credit
assignment during learning.
8
CONCLUSIONS
We presented BEDA , a simple yet general framework that casts be-
lief estimation as probabilistic constraints for executing strategic di-
alogue acts. By formalizing two core acts—Adversarial (introduce
or emphasize events outside the interlocutor’s belief support) and
Alignment (restrict utterances to common ground)—and instanti-
ating them with a world set, dual belief estimators (event truth
and opponent knowledge), and a conditional generator, BEDA
bridges the gap between estimating beliefs and using them dur-
ing generation. Across three settings, Conditional Keeper–Burglar
(adversarial), Mutual Friends (cooperative), and CaSiNo (negoti-
ation), BEDA consistently improves strategic reliability: it raises

success on CKBG (up to +20.6 points with GPT-4.1-nano), improves
success in MF (gains of 4.3–11.2 points, and up to +30.4 in some
configurations) while reducing turns, and negotiates higher-quality
agreements in CaSiNo relative to strong prompting baselines. These
results indicate that constraining generation by inferred belief
structure is an effective organizing principle for dialogue agents,
complementary to generic dialogue heuristics such as CoT or self-
reflection. We see BEDA’s constraint-based view as a step toward
strategic dialogue agents that reason about—and act through—the
beliefs that structure interaction. (See Section A for ethics statement
and societal impacts.)

ETHICS STATEMENT AND SOCIETAL
IMPACT
This work advances agentic AI, aiming to build AI agents capable
of operating in complex social settings. While such agents could,
in principle, produce aggressive utterances, this is neither an ob-
jective nor a desirable behavior in our research, and we try our
best to promise that our technique is designed for the good of hu-
man beings. We mitigate this risk by employing safety-screened,
pre-trained language models, curated training data, and decoding
constraints designed to discourage harmful content. Our study fol-
lows community and institutional ethical guidelines and seeks to
improve reliability, transparency, and responsible deployment of
agentic systems.

E
CONDITIONAL KEEPER-BURGLAR GAME
(CKBG)
E.0.1
The CKBG Task. Derived from the classic "Sally-Anne" psy-
chological experiment [43], Hagendorff [15] proposed the Keeper-
Burglar game to analyze the ability of LLMs to understand the false
beliefs and deception of others. The game is situated in a scenario
containing two containers, one of which conceals a valuable item.
The LLM assumes the role of a Homeowner whose objective is to
mislead an intruding Burglar in order to protect the valuable posses-
sion. To enhance the game’s diversity and simulate more realistic
dialogue scenarios, we extended the original "first-order/second-
order" framework by incorporating additional contextual elements.
These include characters inspecting the containers and the presence
of audible container noises, creating a more dynamic and complex
interaction environment. Therefore, it’s named the Conditional
Keeper-Burglar Game (CKBG).
E.0.2
Construction of CKBG Dataset. The conditions were ran-
domly assigned to both the keeper and the burglar. The five condi-
tion classes are defined as follows:
• Informer: An individual has informed the burglar that the
keeper may be deceitful.
• Burglar inspection: The burglar opened [Container x] [Time]
hours ago and observed [Object x] inside.
• Keeper inspection: The keeper opened [Container x] [Time]
hours ago.
• Outsider inspection: A friend of the keeper opened [Con-
tainer x] [Time] hours ago.
• Noise: A noise was heard emanating from [Container x].
The dataset statistics are presented in Table 5. The conditions
were randomly assigned to both the keeper and the burglar, yielding
a total of 450 condition instances for the test dataset.

Selection Process. Recall the definition of the framework pre-
sented in Section 2. The belief estimation predicts the probabilistic
beliefs across five distinct conditions. The self’s belief reflects the
keeper’s confidence regarding the occurrence of an event, while the
opponent’s belief represents the burglar’s confidence in the same
events. Since the keeper is provided with a list of conditions, we
denote the self’s beliefs on these conditions as 1 (known) and others
as 0 (unknown). Furthermore, a neural network, which models the
opponent’s beliefs, will predict the burglar’s beliefs based on the
ongoing conversation. Subsequently, events for which the burglar’s
belief is 0 will be selected as inputs to the conditional generator for
further generation.
E.0.4
Experimental Details. The experiments are conducted within
a first-person, agent-centric framework, wherein two agents pow-
ered by large language models (LLMs) are assigned the roles of
"keeper" and "burglar" to engage in interactive dialogues. During
prompt construction, each agent is initially provided with role-
specific background information and task objectives via the system
prompt. Additionally, the outputs of a belief estimation module are
incorporated into the system prompt as supplementary contextual
information. To preserve conversational coherence and ensure con-
sistent role alignment, the dialogue history between the two agents
is interleaved as alternating user and assistant turns within the
prompt sequence.
Belief estimator Training. We implement the belief estimator
using a BERT-based architecture. The technical pipeline proceeds
as follows:
• Sentence Encoding: Each utterance in the dialogue is inde-
pendently encoded using BERT, with the final-layer [CLS]
token embedding extracted as the utterance-level represen-
tation.
• Context-Event Fusion: The representations of all utter-
ances are concatenated with event embeddings and passed
through a self-attention layer to facilitate information inte-
gration.
• Binary Classification: At each event-specific position within
the fused representation, the corresponding embedding is
fed into a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with softmax activa-
tion, producing a probabilistic output that indicates whether
the burglar is aware of the particular event.

To better simulate the conditions of actual conversations in the
training and test sets, we randomly clipped the final few rounds of
each multi-turn conversation. This approach ensures that the belief
estimator is trained and tested with less contextual information,
thereby enhancing its adaptability to real-world scenarios.

We depict the details of performing the MF task here.
F.0.1
The MF task. The task of identifying mutual friends between
two interlocutors, referred to as MF, was proposed by He et al.
[16] and provides an ideal framework for examining the alignment
of dialogue acts, where cooperation plays a pivotal role. In this
task, two players are each given a list of friends, with each friend
having a distinct set of attributes, such as name, hobbies, and school.
Importantly, there is exactly one friend common to both players.
The Large Language Model (LLM) is tasked with role-playing both
players, engaging in a dialogue to exchange information over a
limited number of rounds. The game is considered successfully
completed when both players simultaneously confirm the identity
of the mutual friend and select the same individual.

Experimental Details. We developed a symmetric two-agent
experimental framework in which two large language model (LLM)
agents act as players collaborating toward a common objective. Each
agent possesses a distinct friend list, but both share the same game
goal. The initial game context, including the background and friend
lists, is provided through a system prompt. During the dialogue
phase, the conversation history is structured by alternating between
user and assistant roles within the prompt sequence, ensuring both
conversational coherence and consistent role adherence throughout
the interaction. At the end of the dialogue, conditional generation is
employed to assess whether a mutual friend has been identified, and
this judgment is appended to the dialogue history, also presented
as a system prompt to the LLM. The evaluation phase commences
once both players either confirm the identification of a mutual
friend or reach the maximum dialogue turn limit (20 turns). During
this phase, the system prompts each player to select a mutual friend
from their respective friend lists. If both players select the same
individual, the outcome is deemed a success.
Belief estimator Training. We implement the belief estimator
using a BERT-based architecture. The estimation of the belief state
is essentially a binary classification of the utterance-attribute-value
triple. The technical pipeline proceeds as follows:
• Input wrapping: The utterance-attribute-value triple will be
concatenated into a long sentence, separated by [SEP], begin
with [CLS]. For example: "[CLS] [utterance] [SEP] [attribute]
[SEP] [value] [SEP]"
• Sentence Encoding: Each sentence is independently encoded
through BERT, with the final-layer [CLS] token embedding
extracted as the triple-level representation.
• Binary Classification: For each triple, the sentence represen-
tation vector is fed into an MLP with softmax activation,
generating a probabilistic output indicating whether the at-
tribute of the mutual friend, as perceived by the other party,
matches the given value.
We have two GPT-3.5 agents play the game on the training set
of the Mutual Friends dataset (which samples 1,000 data points) to
generate training data for the belief estimator. After each player
speaks, the system asks them to provide the attribute values of the
mutual friend they currently believe in, which serves as the ground
truth label for the training data. To better align with the testing sce-
nario, this ground truth label is fed into the conditional generation
module. For each utterance-attribute-value triple labeled as correct,
we randomly alter either the attribute or the value to construct a
negative sample. The training data maintains a 1:1 ratio between
positive and negative samples. Ultimately, the 1,000 data points
sampled from the original training set generated 67,690 training
samples. We split the data into training, testing, and validation sets
in a 0.9:0.05:0.05 ratio. The belief estimator achieved an accuracy
of 90.1% on the test set.

Selection Process. In the CaSiNo framework, a mixed strategy
is employed, where the conditional generation model is provided
with two distinct events: one corresponding to the conditions of
the Alignment Dialogue Act and the other to the conditions of the
Adversarial Dialogue Act . For the Alignment Dialogue Act , only
the first 12 conditions in the world set are considered relevant, with
the remaining 12 conditions deemed inconsequential. Conversely,
for the Adversarial Dialogue Act , only the last 12 conditions in
the world set are taken into account. The underlying rationale
for the Alignment Dialogue Act is as follows: "I know you prefer
xxx, and I am aware that you are cognizant of your preference
for xxx" (assuming mutual awareness of preferences). In contrast,
the rationale for the Adversarial Dialogue Act is: "I know that I
do not prefer xxx, and I am aware that you are unaware of my
lack of preference for xxx" (anticipating the opponent’s lack of
knowledge).

Experimental Details. We follow the same prompt strategy
as in the experiments of the Conditional Keeper-Burglar Game,
in which the two LLM-based agents are required to assume the
roles of two negotiators; we undertake expanded experimental
investigations on the CaSiNo dataset to systematically evaluate the
influence of belief estimation mechanisms on the game of camping
negotiations.
The classification result of the belief estimator is shown in Ta-
ble 4, we evaluate the accuracy of the belief estimator through a
fine-grained analytical framework: For each permutation of the
three resources, which inherently contains three pairwise com-
parative relationships (𝐶2
3 = 3 combinations). So among all the 6
permutations, we have:
• Only one permutation is entirely correct (all three pairwise
relationships accurate);
• Only one permutation is entirely erroneous (no accurate
pairwise relationships);
• Two permutations retain two accurate pairwise relation-
ships;
• Two permutations preserve a single accurate pairwise rela-
tionship.
We define fine-grained accuracy as the ratio of accurate pairwise
relationships for every single piece of data. The belief estimator
does a 6-class classification to select the best permutations for both
2 negotiators.

DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORKS
Apply an LLM for belief estimation. A smaller model is more
efficient than an LLM, and our experimental results have shown
that a simple encoder already has the capability of capturing beliefs
from conversations. It’s possible to use an LLM as belief estimator,
but we consider a smaller model is enough.
Using a weak instruct following model. As is noticed, LlaMA not
been used to conduct experiments on the Mutual Friends (MF)
dataset. Our experimental setup differs from [33] in that no super-
vised fine-tuning was applied to the LLMs. We demonstrate that the
unmodified llama model cannot perform the Mutual Friends (MF)
game. Based on Llama-3.1-8b, Llama-2-13b, and Llama-2-7b, we
conducted preliminary experiments by sampling 50 data points
from the test set. In five experimental settings—w/o belief, rand
belief, cot, self-reflect, and BEDA —the success rates did not exceed
5%. This outcome is attributed to the larger World Set in the Mutual
Friend task, where Llama fails to extract meaningful information
from the structured friend list, leading to significant hallucinations
during generation. An example was shown in Table 13.
Dynamic Construction of the World Set. This work primarily
focuses on bridging belief estimation with dialogue acts. Future
research will explore the development of a dynamic world set.
