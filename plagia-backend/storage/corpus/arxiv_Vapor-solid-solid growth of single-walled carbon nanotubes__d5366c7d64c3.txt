Vapor-solid-solid growth of single-walled carbon nanotubes
Daniel Hedman1, ∗
1Center for Multidimensional Carbon Materials (CMCM),
Institute for Basic Science (IBS), Ulsan, 44919, Republic of Korea
(Dated: December 30, 2025)
Single-walled carbon nanotubes are one-dimensional sp2 carbon materials whose electronic and
optical properties are governed by their chirality. Catalytic chemical vapor deposition often uses
transition-metal nanoparticles that liquefy at elevated temperature, and vapor-liquid-solid growth is
commonly associated with broad chirality distributions. Improved selectivity has been reported for
high-melting-point catalysts that remain solid, suggesting vapor-solid-solid growth, but the under-
lying kinetics and interface structure remain poorly resolved. The mechanisms that control carbon
delivery and determine edge structure on solid catalysts are therefore unclear. Here it is shown, us-
ing microsecond-scale molecular dynamics driven by a neuroevolution machine-learning interatomic
potential, that rhenium nanoparticles remain solid above 1123.15 K and that surface carbon at
5.0 nm−2 to 6.0 nm−2 does not appreciably depress melting.
Carbon transport is dominated by
facet-dependent surface diffusion, bounding sustainable supply on a 2.0 nm particle to ∼44 carbon
atoms per µs on the slow (10¯11) facet.
Consistently, growth at 50 carbon atoms per µs occurs
only within a narrow window: multiple nucleation or encapsulation is promoted at 1300 K, tubular
elongation is obtained at 1400 K, and low-curvature graphitic structures dominate at 1500 K and
above. Non-hexagonal rings persist over 12 µs, while zigzag-rich, strongly Klein-decorated edges are
stabilized and deviate from configurational-entropy expectations for liquid catalysts. These results
place catalyst reconstruction by surface carbon adsorption, facet-controlled diffusion, and crystalline
interfacial thermodynamics at the center of vapor-solid-solid growth control, motivating experimen-
tal tuning of temperature and feedstock partial pressure to balance diffusion-limited supply against
encapsulation pathways.
Keywords: machine-learning interatomic potential; molecular dynamics; vapor-solid-solid growth; single-
walled carbon nanotubes
I.
INTRODUCTION
Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are hollow cylinders of sp2-
bonded carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattisce.
For single-walled CNTs (SWCNTs), electronic and opti-
cal properties are set by their chirality, described by two
chiral indices (n, m) that specify the orientation of the
lattice relative to the tube axis. Chirality is established
during SWCNT-cap nucleation but can be modified by
the introduction of defects, non-hexagonal rings (e.g., pen-
tagons and heptagons). Catalytic chemical vapor deposi-
tion (CCVD) remains the most common synthesis route for
SWCNTs and typically employs transition-metal nanopar-
ticles (NPs) as catalysts. For common catalysts such as
iron [1] and nickel [2], as well as alloys such as nickel-iron
[3], the catalyst is frequently liquid under growth condi-
tions because of nanoscale melting-point depression com-
bined with the elevated temperatures used in CCVD (often
above 800 K). Under such conditions, growth is commonly
described by a vapor-liquid-solid (VLS) mechanism [4] and
tends to yield broad chirality distributions [1, 3, 5–7], re-
sulting in correspondingly broad property distributions.
In contrast, improved chiral and/or diameter selectiv-
ity has been reported for growth using high-melting-point
catalysts that remain solid during CCVD, including re-
fractory metals such as molybdenum, tungsten, and rhe-
nium [8]; carbide NPs such as molybdenum carbide [9]
and tungsten carbide [10]; and refractory alloy NPs such
as cobalt-tungsten [11–13] and cobalt-rhenium [14].
For
these solid-state catalysts, CNT growth has been proposed
to proceed through a vapor-solid-solid (VSS) mechanism
∗daniel@hedman.science
[15] rather than VLS. Machine-learning interatomic poten-
tial (MLIP) driven molecular dynamics (MD) has recently
provided atomistic insight into CNT growth on liquid Fe
[6, 16] and Co [7] catalysts. But comparable mechanistic
understanding remains limited for solid-state catalysts, in
part due to the lack of VSS growth simulations at relevant
length and time scales. The present work addresses this
gap by investigating SWCNT growth on solid-state rhe-
nium catalysts using MLIP-driven MD.
Results support a VSS growth regime by the persistence
of solid rhenium NPs in the experimentally relevant size
range, with melting points well above the growth temper-
ature and only weak dependence on presence of surface
carbon. In this regime, carbon transport is dominated by
surface diffusion rather than bulk dissolution, and diffusion
kinetics are strongly facet-dependent. Sluggish carbon dif-
fusion becomes rate-limiting and imposes a stringent upper
bound on sustainable carbon supply, above which excess
carbon accumulates and promotes encapsulation or com-
peting nucleation pathways. Consistent with this picture,
growth simulations indicate that single cap nucleation and
elongation are confined to a narrow kinetic window: lower
temperatures promote carbon buildup and multiple nu-
cleation, whereas higher temperatures favor low-curvature
graphitic structures and encapsulation, highlighting VSS
growth control by coupled diffusion and defect-healing time
scales. Cap nucleation is observed on terraces and at junc-
tions between terraces, and the nascent cap/edge com-
monly anchors near facet edges where under-coordinated
metal atoms stabilize attachment. Subsequent elongation
proceeds with slow defect healing and persistent incorpo-
ration of non-hexagonal rings, indicating long-lived inter-
facial defects. Equilibrium simulations further show that
solid rhenium NPs suppresses the configurational-entropy-
driven edge structures observed for VLS growth, stabilizing

II.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Machine-learning interatomic potentials enable atom-
istic simulations at length and time scales approaching
experiment by learning energies, forces, and virials from
first-principles reference data (typically density functional
theory, DFT). After training, MLIPs can be used to drive
molecular dynamics with computational cost comparable
to empirical force fields while retaining near-DFT accuracy.
A central challenge in MLIP development is the construc-
tion of training datasets that are both high quality and
sufficiently diverse.
Here, an Re-C dataset was generated by active learn-
ing and is summarized by the sketch-map representation
in Fig.
1.
Each point corresponds to a unique atomic
configuration, positioned by principal component analysis
of learned descriptors of local atomic environments. The
observed clustering indicates that structurally similar con-
figurations map to nearby regions while dissimilar configu-
rations separate, consistent with broad coverage of config-
urations relevant to SWCNT growth on rhenium.
A neuroevolution potential (NEP) [17] was trained on
this dataset using a 90%, 10% split into training and valida-
tion sets. As shown in Fig. S1, NEPRe-C yields energy and
force errors of 11.44 meV per atom and 398.7 meV ˚A
−1, re-
spectively. Additional verification of NEPRe-C is provided
in Sec. 1 of the Supporting Information.
The resulting potential is computationally efficient and
enables 100 ns to 150 ns per day of simulated CNT growth,
depending on system size, using GPUMD on an NVIDIA
T4 GPU. Under these conditions, 1 µs of growth can be
simulated in approximately 7 days.
For vapor-solid-solid (VSS) growth, the catalyst is re-
quired to remain solid during growth. Because SWCNT
growth typically proceeds on very small nanoparticles-
to avoid multi-walled CNTs and carbon nanofibers-the
nanoparticle melting point, Tm, must be established at
the relevant sizes. Melting simulations of polycrystalline,
icosahedral and Wulff rhenium NPs were performed using
NEPRe-C. As shown in Fig. 2c, even very small N = 55
atom (∼1 nm) rhenium NPs exhibit melting points exceed-
ing 1000 K. For NP sizes closer to those used by Zhang
et al.
[8] to experimentally grow SWCNTs on rhenium
(∼1.8 nm, diameter), the melting point exceeds 2000 K.
Thus, at the growth temperature used by Zhang et al.
(1123.15 K), growth is expected to proceed via VSS.
A separation in melting behavior is observed for NPs
larger than ∼2.5 nm, where icosahedral NPs display signif-
icantly lower melting points than Wulff-shaped NPs (Fig.
2c). This trend is consistent with Wulff NPs having lower
free energy than icosahedral NPs above ∼2.0 nm diameter
(Fig. S2). During the melting simulations, polycrystalline
NPs anneal and evolve toward structures similar to the
Wulff NPs (SI Video 1).
Because the solubility of carbon in rhenium is very
low [19], carbon atoms are expected to reside predomi-
nantly on the NP surface during growth rather than dis-
solve into the bulk. The effect of surface carbon on the
melting point was therefore investigated.
As shown in
Fig.
2d, surface carbon does not significantly affect Tm
up to concentrations of CC = 7.0 nm−2. During SWCNT
growth, the surface-carbon concentration is approximately
CC = 5.0 nm−2 to 6.0 nm−2 (shown below); therefore, the
melting point of the NPs is not expected to be significantly
affected by the presence of surface carbon under growth
conditions.
A central distinction between vapor-liquid-solid (VLS)
and VSS growth is the pathway by which carbon is trans-
ported to the growing nanotube. In VLS growth, carbon
transport in the liquid catalyst proceeds through diffusion
and advection. In VSS growth, carbon transport can occur
through bulk and surface diffusion; however, for rhenium,
the low carbon solubility implies that transport is domi-
nated by surface diffusion.
To quantify this effect, surface-carbon diffusion was eval-
uated on the facets that comprise the rhenium Wulff
particles.
Two Wulff constructs were considered:
a
“clean” shape at zero surface carbon (Fig.
2a) and a
carbon-covered shape representative of growth conditions
at CC = 5.5 nm−2 (Fig. 2b). The predicted equilibrium
shape changes markedly upon carbon adsorption. Because
CCVD growth is out of equilibrium and is typically initi-
ated from a comparatively clean nanoparticle, the instan-
taneous catalyst shape during growth is expected to lie
between these limits (discussed below). Nevertheless, the
facet set in Fig. 2a,b provides a consistent basis for com-
paring surface-diffusion kinetics.
As shown in Fig. 2e, diffusion coefficients vary substan-
tially across facets, with differences of approximately one to
two orders of magnitude between the fastest facet (0001)
and the slowest facet (10¯11), depending on temperature.

Be-
cause the (10¯11) facet dominates the carbon-covered rhe-
nium NP during growth (Fig. 2b), carbon delivery to the
CNT edge is expected to be limited by diffusion on (10¯11)
and therefore to be approximately 50 times slower than
carbon diffusion in liquid Fe.
The consequences of sluggish surface diffusion can be es-
timated using a spherical model. For a d = 2.0 nm diameter
particle, a characteristic equator-to-pole diffusion distance
L = dπ/4 and a surface diffusion coefficient equal to that of
(10¯11) at 1500 K, D1500 K
(10¯11) = 0.027 nm2/ns, yield a charac-
teristic transport time L2/(4D1500 K
(10¯11) ) = 23 ns. Under the
idealized assumptions that incorporation at the CNT edge
is instantaneous and that carbon does not leave the NP,
this time corresponds to an upper bound of approximately
44 carbon atoms per µs. Carbon supply substantially ex-
ceeding this value is expected to cause carbon buildup, pro-
moting competing nucleation events or encapsulation.
For comparison, in VLS growth on liquid iron, the lim-
iting rate for producing long, defect-free tubes has been
linked to defect formation and healing [6].
At 1500 K,
growth rates below 750 carbon atoms per µs yielded long
defect-free tubes [6], more than an order of magnitude
larger than the diffusion-limited estimate for rhenium (44
carbon atoms per µs).
The slow carbon transport intrinsic to VSS growth is ex-
pected to influence both nucleation and elongation on solid
catalysts. Growth simulations were therefore performed on
a Re594 Wulff NP at a carbon supply rate of k = 50 carbon
per µs, chosen to be close to the diffusion-limited estimate
discussed above.
In practice, simulation throughput constrained the ac-
cessible parameter space. Using NEPRe-C with GPUMD,
growth simulations on Re594 progress at approximately
150 ns per day.
At k = 50 carbon per µs, this corre-
sponds to insertion of roughly seven carbon atoms per day.
Consequently, complete growth simulations require multi-
ple weeks even when initiated from a prepared Re594 NP
preloaded with surface carbon, underscoring that further
improvements in computational efficiency remain desirable.
To map growth behavior efficiently, shorter simulations
of 6 µs (300 carbon atoms added) were first performed
to assess the influence of temperature. Simulations were
conducted at 1300 K, 1400 K, 1500 K and 1600 K with
five independent runs at each temperature.

Similar
outcomes were reported for VLS growth on liquid iron
at high temperature and were attributed to shorter life-
times of interfacial defects [6].
In particular, reduced
pentagon-defect lifetimes alter the ratio of pentagons to
hexagons, N5/N6, thereby lowering the Gaussian curvature
(∝N5/N6) and favoring low-curvature graphitic morpholo-
gies. Therefore, although increasing temperature acceler-
ates diffusion, excessive temperature promotes encapsula-
tion and suppresses SWCNT growth.
Because growth at 1400 K and k = 50 carbon per µs
yielded sustained tubular growth in the short tests, longer
simulations of 12 µs (600 carbon atoms added) were per-
formed under the same conditions. Figures 4a,b show one
representative trajectory that produces a well-defined, al-
beit defective, SWCNT.

Cap nucleation initiates at 2.16 µs on the (0001) facet

This

In the trajectory shown, the initial ring formation pro-

of the CNT edge. Such behavior has also been reported
experimentally in in situ TEM growth of SWCNTs on solid
cobalt-tungsten, where the growing cap appeared anchored
at a facet edge [20].
Across the five 12 µs growth simulations, cap nucleation
occurred on (0001) in two trajectories, on (10¯11) in one
trajectory, and at an interface between two facets in two
trajectories. After nucleation, the cap continued to grow
and extended beyond the initial nucleation facet (Fig. 4b,
3.73 µs to 12.00 µs). In the representative case shown, the

edge of the (10¯11) facet to attach to the growing edge, form-

SI Video 3). Therefore, growth is not necessarily confined
to the facet on which nucleation initiates.

Although the representative growth trajectory yields a
defective tube, the associated evolution of the SWCNT
edge provides insight into solid-catalyst interfacial kinet-
ics. As shown in Fig. 5a (top), the edge length increases
continuously during growth, consistent with ongoing incor-
poration of interfacial defects (Fig. 4d). Nevertheless, the
edge composition remains strongly biased toward zigzag
(ZZ) sites.
When the numbers of armchair (AC) pairs,
NAC, and ZZ sites, NZZ, are normalized by the total num-
ber of edge sites, NAC + NZZ, ZZ sites account for an aver-
age of 64% (Fig. 5a, middle). This preference is consistent
with the previously reported large difference between AC
and ZZ interface energies for rhenium, 0.33 eV per atom
[21].
In addition, both AC and ZZ edge motifs can be ter-
minated by an extra carbon atom (a Klein atom) rather
than a metal atom [22]. The fractions of Klein-decorated
AC and ZZ sites (KAC and KZZ, respectively) are shown
in Fig. 5a (bottom). On average, 45% of edge atoms are
Klein-decorated. Klein decoration is strongly motif depen-
dent: only 28% of AC edge atoms are KAC on average,
whereas 63% of ZZ edge atoms are KZZ on average. The
preference for ZZ and KZZ is also apparent in snapshots of
Fig. 4b and SI Video 4.

For SWCNTs on liquid NPs with a small to moderate
difference between AC and ZZ interface energies, αAC(θ)
is expected to follow the configurational-entropy model of
Bichara et al. [16, 23, 24], exemplified by equilibrium re-
sults for SWCNTs on liquid iron at 1300 K (orange line in
Fig. 5b) [25]. In contrast, ⟨αAC⟩on both the (0001) and
(10¯11) facets deviates from the configurational-entropy ex-
pectation. This deviation is attributed to the strong ener-
getic preference for ZZ bonding on rhenium and the crys-
talline nature of the catalyst surface, which cannot conform
as readily to arbitrary edge structures as a liquid NP. The
suppression of AC pairs is most pronounced for θ ≥18◦,
where ⟨αAC⟩is substantially lower than for tubes attached
to liquid iron, and yields highly tilted interfaces in the an-
nealed structures (Fig. 5c).
For these tilted, ZZ-rich interfaces, KZZ termination is
prevalent. Averaging over the final 500 ns indicates that,
on both facets, more than 50% of edge atoms are Klein-
decorated with only weak chirality dependence (Fig. 5d).
Motif-resolved analysis shows that a large fraction (greater
than 80% on average) of ZZ atoms are KZZ, whereas fewer
than 40% (average) of AC atoms are KAC, again with only
weak chirality dependence. A modest facet dependence is
also observed, with tubes on (0001) exhibiting a stronger
preference for Klein decoration for both AC and ZZ motifs.
MLIP-driven simulations indicate that VSS growth dif-
fers from VLS growth through sluggish carbon transport
governed by facet-dependent surface diffusion and through
suppression of configurational-entropy-driven edge struc-
tures. For rhenium, these effects manifest as slow growth
and defect-healing kinetics together with stabilization of
zigzag-rich, strongly Klein-decorated edges over a broad
chirality range.

supply rates not accessible to the MLIP-driven growth sim-

NP surface was approximated as a sphere with a diffu-

north pole (aligned with the z axis) and treated as a carbon
sink. The surface area covered by the tube had zero carbon
supply, whereas the remaining exposed surface acted as a
uniform carbon source so that the sink and source, S(ψ),
are balanced.

on (10¯11) (slow diffusion), as shown in Fig. 6a,b. Using a
sink rate equal to the carbon supply rate in the growth sim-
ulations, k = 50 carbon per µs, the model yields distinct
steady state excess concentrations (Fig. 6c). Higher excess
concentration and sharper gradients are obtained when the
tube grows on (10¯11) than when growth occurs on (0001).
This difference is reflected in the time evolution of the ex-
cess number of carbon atoms relative to equilibrium (Fig.
6d), which reaches a higher steady state value for growth
on the slow-diffusion facet.
In the absence of a sink (no growing CNT), a uniformly
distributed source produces a uniform surface concentra-
tion. Therefore, the facet on which a CNT nucleates and
grows strongly affects the distribution of excess carbon else-
where on the particle: when growth proceeds on a facet
with slow diffusion, excess carbon is predicted to accumu-
late more strongly on regions with faster diffusion, which
can promote additional nucleation events or encapsulation.
The excess carbon population also depends on the car-
bon supply rate, which is controlled experimentally by
the feedstock partial pressure.
As shown by Eq.
1 and
Fig. 6e, the excess number of carbon atoms is linear in k
but nonlinear in T. Thus, to maintain low excess carbon
and suppress multiple nucleation or encapsulation at lower
growth temperatures, a substantially lower carbon supply
rate is required for VSS growth than for VLS growth, par-
ticularly when growth proceeds on a slow-diffusion facet.
These results motivate experimental reduction of the car-
bon supply rate, for example by lowering the feedstock par-
tial pressure, to enable high-quality SWCNT growth under
diffusion-limited VSS conditions.
III.
CONCLUSIONS
Microsecond-scale, machine-learning interatomic poten-
tial driven molecular dynamics was used to investigate
vapor-solid-solid (VSS) growth of single-walled carbon nan-
otubes (SWCNTs) on solid-state rhenium nanoparticles.
The trained NEPRe-C enabled direct simulation of nu-
cleation and early elongation while retaining near-first-
principles fidelity. Across experimentally relevant nanopar-
ticle sizes, melting points were found to exceed the growth
temperature of 1123.15 K, and surface carbon at concen-
trations typical of growth (5.0 nm−2 to 6.0 nm−2) did not
appreciably depress melting, supporting a solid-catalyst
growth regime.
Carbon transport on rhenium was shown to be domi-
nated by facet-dependent surface diffusion, with diffusion
coefficients differing by one to two orders of magnitude
across Wulff facets and being substantially slower than
carbon diffusion in liquid iron. A diffusion-limited upper
bound on sustainable supply was obtained for a 2.0 nm par-
ticle, yielding 23 ns for equator-to-pole transport on the
slow (10¯11) facet at 1500 K and an associated bound of
∼44 carbon atoms per µs. Growth simulations at a sup-
ply rate of 50 carbon per µs revealed a narrow kinetic win-
dow: at 1300 K, sluggish diffusion promoted carbon ac-
cumulation and multiple nucleation or encapsulation; at
1400 K, single-cap nucleation and tubular elongation were
obtained; and at 1500 K and above, low-curvature graphitic
structures consistent with encapsulation dominated, con-
sistent with accelerated defect removal suppressing the cur-
vature required for cap stability.
Microsecond-scale growth trajectories indicated that cap

8
nucleation occurred on terraces and at junctions between
facets, and that the nascent cap/edge frequently anchored
near facet edges where under-coordinated metal atoms sta-
bilize attachment. Subsequent elongation proceeded with
persistent incorporation of non-hexagonal rings and slow
defect healing, with continuous accumulation of pentagons
and heptagons over 12 µs at 1400 K. Despite this kinetic
defect accumulation, the interfacial edge composition re-
mained strongly biased toward zigzag motifs with sub-
stantial Klein decoration during growth. Equilibrium an-
nealing further showed that, on crystalline rhenium facets,
the armchair-pair fraction deviated from configurational-
entropy expectations for liquid catalysts and stabilized
zigzag-rich, strongly Klein-decorated edges across a broad
chiral-angle range.
A simplified diffusion model extended these observa-
tions beyond the explicitly simulated conditions and con-
nected excess surface-carbon accumulation to the coupled
effects of temperature, carbon supply rate, and the facet
on which a tube nucleates. Together, the results identify
solid-catalyst reconstruction by surface carbon adsorption,
facet-controlled surface diffusion, and solid-state interfacial
thermodynamics as central determinants of VSS growth ki-
netics and edge structure. These findings motivate exper-
imental control through temperature and feedstock par-
tial pressure to balance diffusion-limited carbon delivery
against defect-mediated encapsulation and competing nu-
cleation pathways.

The Neuroevolution potential (NEP) v4 [17] was trained
using GPUMD version 3.9.4.
Training data were gener-

(Fig. S1). An ensemble of four NEPs was used to iden-
tify configurations encountered during molecular dynam-
ics (MD) that were outside the distribution of the current
training set by monitoring the deviation in the predicted
forces (model deviation).
For a given configuration, the model deviation σF was
defined as the maximum force sample standard deviation
over all atoms,

The newly labeled configurations were ap-

Active learning was performed for 18 iterations to reach
an acceptable model deviation for test growth simulations.
The final dataset comprised 96 023 images (Fig. 1) and
was split into 90% training and 10% validation.

B.
Data labeling
Each structure in the training dataset was labeled by
single-point DFT calculations to obtain energies, forces,
and virials.
Calculations were performed using the Vi-
enna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) [26–28] ver-
sion 6.3.2.
A plane-wave basis was employed together
with the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method [29,
30] and standard pseudopotentials (Re 17Jan2003 and C
08Apr2002).
Labeling was performed in two steps. First, loose DFT
settings were used with the local density approximation
(LDA) exchange-correlation functional and large initial
magnetic moments of 5 e−for rhenium and 2 e−for carbon.
Direct minimization using the preconditioned conjugate-
gradient method (ALGO = Conjugate) was applied to ob-
tain reliable magnetic moments. Second, these magnetic
moments were used as the initial guess for calculations
with tighter settings.
The rev-vdW-DF2 van der Waals
exchange-correlation functional [31, 32] was used with non-
spherical gradient corrections enabled (LASPH = True). A
plane-wave cutoff energy of 700 eV was applied (ENCUT =
700) and symmetry was disabled (ISYM = 0). The elec-
tronic self-consistent loop was converged to 1 × 10−6 eV
(EDIFF = 1.0E-6). Gaussian smearing was used (ISMEAR
= 0) with 0.05 eV smearing width (SIGMA = 0.05). Spin
polarization was enabled (ISPIN = 2) using the magnetic
moments obtained from the first step. For periodic struc-
tures, a Γ-centered k-point mesh with density π/10 ˚A
−1

9
was used (KSPACING = 0.3142). For non-periodic struc-
tures, only the Γ point was used with at least 15 ˚A vacuum
spacing between periodic images.
C.
Molecular dynamics
All MD simulations were performed using Graphics Pro-
cessing Units Molecular Dynamics (GPUMD) [33, 34] ver-
sion 3.9.4.
One of the four trained NEPs (Sec.
IV A)
was used to propagate each MD trajectory. The ensem-
ble model deviation was evaluated every 10.0 ps as a mea-
sure of trajectory reliability. All simulations used a 2.0 fs
timestep and a Nos´e-Hoover chain thermostat with chain
length 4.
a.
Nanoparticle melting simulations.
To determine
the melting points in Fig.
2, nanoparticles were heated
from 1000 K to 3500 K at 25 K ns−1.
Wulff nanoparti-
cles were constructed using DFT-calculated surface ener-
gies with and without a surface-carbon concentration of
5.5 nm−2 for each of the 12 rhenium HCP facets available
in the Crystalium database [35]. Polycrystalline nanoparti-
cles were obtained by first cooling from 3500 K to 1000 K at
25 K ns−1 prior to heating. The melting point Tm of each
nanoparticle was identified from the discontinuity (first-
order phase transition) in the potential energy during heat-
ing.
b.
Surface diffusion simulations.
Surface-diffusion co-
efficients for carbon on rhenium HCP facets were computed
using 5 nm × 5 nm supercell slabs of nine facets: (0001),
(10¯10), (11¯20), (21¯30), (10¯11), (10¯12), (21¯31), (11¯21), and
(2¯1¯12).
Carbon atoms were randomly deposited on one
side of the slab to reach a surface-carbon concentration
of 5.5 nm−2. Each system was equilibrated for 10.0 ns in
the NPT ensemble at ambient pressure 0.1 MPa using a
Nos´e-Hoover chain thermostat and a Parrinello-Rahman
barostat. Production simulations were then performed for
100 ns in the NVT ensemble. Mean-squared displacements
of carbon atoms were recorded every 2.0 ps and were used
to extract diffusion coefficients.
c.
SWCNT growth simulations.
SWCNT growth sim-
ulations were performed on Re594 Wulff nanoparticles by
inserting single carbon atoms every 20 ns, corresponding
to an insertion (growth) rate of 50 carbon per µs. Carbon
atoms were deposited at random surface locations selected
to avoid unphysical insertion inside the nanotube or near
the CNT/NP interface. A vector was first computed from
the center of position of existing carbon atoms to the cen-
ter of position of rhenium atoms.
A spherical cone was
then defined with apex at the nanoparticle center and axis
along this vector, pointing outward from the nanoparticle
and away from the carbon cluster.
The cone half-angle
was 90◦. A random direction within this cone was sam-
pled and, starting from the nanoparticle center, a point
was marched outward along this direction until the dis-
tance to the nearest rhenium atom exceeded 2.5 ˚A and the
distance to the nearest carbon atom exceeded 1.4 ˚A. A new
carbon atom was then inserted at this position. To avoid
excessive translation and rotation induced by repeated in-
sertions, the total linear and angular momenta of the full
system were zeroed every 50 timesteps.
