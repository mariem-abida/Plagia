Brownian circuits implement computation through stochastic transitions driven by thermal fluc-
tuations. While the energetic costs of such fluctuation-driven computation have been extensively
studied within stochastic thermodynamics, much less is known about its computational complexity,
in particular how computation time scales with circuit size. Here, the computation time of explicitly
designed Brownian circuits is investigated numerically via the first-passage time to a completed state.
For arithmetic circuits such as adders, varying the forward transition rate induces a sharp change
in the scaling behavior of the mean computation time, from linear to exponential in circuit size.
This change can be interpreted as an easy-hard transition in computational time complexity. The
transition suggests that, for meaningful computational tasks, achieving efficient polynomial-time
computation generically requires a finite forward bias, corresponding to a nonzero energy input.
As a counterexample, it is shown that arbitrary logical operations can be reduced to an effectively
one-dimensional stochastic process, for which the zero-bias limit lines within the computationally
efficient (easy) regime. However, realizing such a one-dimensional normal form unavoidably leads
to an exponential increase in circuit size.
These results reveal a fundamental trade-off between
computation time, circuit size, and energy input in Brownian circuits, and demonstrate that phase
transitions in time complexity provide a natural framework for characterizing the cost of fluctuation-
driven computation.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Computation is the process of transforming symbols
according to prescribed rules. These rules, called algo-
rithms, can be implemented in a wide variety of physical
systems. In any such implementation, a computer en-
codes symbols into physical states and carries out com-
putation via the time evolution of those states.
The
physical limits on computation have been discussed from
several perspectives. Feynman emphasized the intrinsic
difficulty of simulating physical phenomena on conven-
tional digital computers and proposed quantum compu-
tation as a form of natural computation [1]. Lloyd, on
the other hand, analyzed fundamental bounds on com-
putational speed arising from limits imposed by quan-
tum mechanics [2]. These studies demonstrate that com-
putational performance is governed by both mathemat-
ical computational models and physical laws, such as
thermodynamics, quantum mechanics, and energy con-
straints. Among these, thermodynamics plays a central
role in constraining computation implemented in noisy,
fluctuation-driven physical systems.
From a thermodynamic viewpoint, computers differ
fundamentally from conventional engines. While engines
convert energy into useful mechanical work, computers
expend energy in order to process information and typi-
cally dissipate most of it as heat. For thermodynamic en-
gines, efficiency limits and trade-offs are rigorously con-
strained by the second law of thermodynamics.
Since
computation is also a physical process, it must conse-
quently obey the second law, implying unavoidable en-
tropy production and fundamental trade-offs among en-
ergy dissipation, speed, and reliability. As a result, ther-
modynamics has provided powerful tools for analyzing
the energy cost of information processing. However, how
these thermodynamic constraints translate into compu-
tational cost in the sense of computational complexity,
namely how computation time scales with problem or
circuit size, remains an open and actively debated prob-
lem [3].
Pioneering work by Landauer and Bennett established
the foundations of the physics of computation by explic-
itly connecting information processing with thermody-
namics.
Landauer argued that information is physical
and that logically irreversible operations necessarily im-
pose a minimum thermodynamic cost, known as Lan-
dauer’s principle [4, 5]. Bennett extended this viewpoint
by investigating computational processes within idealized
physical systems, clarifying how computation can pro-
ceed reliably despite being driven by microscopic fluctu-
ations [6]. This research line emphasized the significant
impact of thermal noise on computational dynamics and
motivated the development of computational models that
are both robust against and driven by such fluctuations,
together with analyses of their computational cost.
The standard mathematical models of computation are
Turing machines and logic circuits. Bennett proposed a
physical realization of such models based on particles or
molecules in Brownian motion [6].
In this framework,
the computational state is encoded in particle configura-
tions and coupling, and the state transitions occur only
when particles meet specific local conditions. The dy-
namics of this system are driven by thermal fluctuations.
Because the transition rules are fixed by the circuit de-
sign, thermal fluctuations do not yield incorrect results;
instead, they induce stochastic forward and backward

motion along valid computational paths.
With a suf-
ficient forward bias, the system eventually reaches the
completed state.
Additionally, extensive research on Brownian motors
and molecular machines has revealed that useful tasks
can be performed autonomously by actively utilizing
thermal fluctuations. In such systems, directional pro-
cessing emerges from stochastic transitions combined
with structural asymmetries, without requiring exter-
nally imposed step-by-step control.
These fluctuation-
driven mechanisms provide a concrete physical basis for
the Brownian computation and demonstrate how ther-
mal fluctuations themselves can serve as computational
resources [7–10]. Related ideas are also being actively in-
vestigated in biological contexts, such as the energy cost
of information processing in living cells, including DNA
replication and transcription.
Such systems are inherently nonequilibrium.
In re-
cent years, information thermodynamics has been de-
veloped as a framework for systematically and quan-
titatively describing the interplay between information
processing and thermodynamics [11–15].
Within this
framework, it has been clarified that entropy produc-
tion and work associated with fundamental information-
processing operations, such as measurement, memory
operations, and feedback control, can be expressed in
terms of information-theoretic quantities. Furthermore,
advances in stochastic thermodynamics have yielded uni-
versal inequalities relating speed, precision, and energy
consumption in stochastic processes, including the fluc-
tuation theorem and the thermodynamic uncertainty re-
lation. Related analyses have also been applied to com-
putational models driven by Brownian motion [16–22].
While stochastic thermodynamics has thus clarified
fundamental costs and universal trade-offs associated
with computational processes, it has not directly ad-
dressed computational cost from the viewpoint of compu-
tational complexity theory. In computational complexity
theory, computational cost is defined through its scaling
with the size of a problem instance, which determines the
intrinsic hardness of the computation. By contrast, many
previous thermodynamic studies have focused on trade-
offs or lower bounds derived from inequalities governing
the progression of physical processes at fixed scales. As
a result, the role of thermodynamic constraints on the
scaling behavior of computation time with circuit size
remains an open question.
In this study, we examine Brownian computation from
a computational-complexity viewpoint by numerically
evaluating the computation time of an explicitly designed
Brownian circuit that implements concrete arithmetic
operations. By analyzing several types of adder circuits,
we observe that varying the forward transition rates leads
to a qualitative change in the scaling behavior of the
mean computation time. This change, from linear to ex-
ponential scaling with circuit size, can be interpreted as
an easy-hard transition.
At a theoretical level, this behavior can be understood
by reducing the dynamics to a one-dimensional first-
passage problem, for which the existence of such a tran-
sition is well established. However, our results also reveal
an important limitation. Namely, even when a Brown-
ian circuit can be reduced to an effective one-dimensional
process and thus exhibit a clear transition, achieving this
reduction may require an exponentially large circuit. In
such cases, the apparent gain in dynamical efficiency is
compensated by an exponential space cost.
These observations emphasize a fundamental trade-off
between time, space, and energy in the context of Brow-
nian computation. While similar trade-offs are familiar
from thermodynamic considerations, our analysis sug-
gests that they can also be derived from a computational
complexity perspective by focusing on the scaling of com-
putational time with circuit size. This complexity-based
viewpoint provides a complementary framework for un-
derstanding the performance limits of Brownian circuits
beyond purely thermodynamic bounds.
This paper is organized as follows. Sec. II introduces
the computational model of Brownian circuits, including
the CJoin gate and the definition of computational cost.
Sec. III describes circuit design principles, with a dis-
cussion of modular architectures such as adders and the
construction of general circuits using the sum-of-products
form. Sec. IV develops a theoretical framework for an-
alyzing Brownian circuits through their state-transition
diagrams, including one-dimensional first-passage anal-
ysis, an embedding method for general graphs, and a
conjecture on easy-hard transitions. Sec. V presents nu-
merical simulations of first-passage times, demonstrating
phase transitions in modular adders, as well as the ab-
sence of such transitions in certain architectures. Finally,
Sec. VI discusses the implications of these results for com-
putational costs, circuit design, and the role of energy in-
put, and concludes the paper. Technical derivations and
additional examples are provided in the Appendices.
II.
SETUP AND COMPUTATIONAL COST OF
BROWNIAN CIRCUITS
This section introduces the fundamental components
of Brownian circuits and discusses their computational
cost. We begin by describing the basic building blocks
that support particle movement and logical operations.
Next, we examine how these elements form a state-
transition diagram representing the dynamic behavior of
the circuit. Finally, we discuss how to measure the com-
putational cost, focusing on how circuit size and structure
affect overall computational performance.

Logic Gate and CJoin Gate

structed using Brownian particles, pathways (referred to
as “wires”), and gates. Brownian particles move along

Gates define transitions between these states by enabling
particles to move from one state to another along the

particles themselves is not necessary. Instead, the cir-
cuit’s logical operations are realized through the careful
design of wires and gates.
We use the Conservative Join (CJoin) gate as its pri-
mary component [23, 24].
The CJoin gate consists of
two pairs of wires, as shown in Fig. 1. The gate has four
wires – two inputs and two outputs – grouped into two
pairs. A transition occurs when two particles simulta-
neously occupy one pair of wires, causing them to move
to the other pair. To build a Brownian circuit, CJoin
gates are interconnected via branching wires, allowing
particles on a wire to interact with multiple connected
gates. Since each gate’s transition depends only on the
presence of two particles in the corresponding wire pair,
wire bifurcations do not interfere with logical operations.
Even when gates and wires are entirely functional, cer-
tain factors may interfere with circuit operations. For
example, multiple particles may occupy a single wire, or
a gate could become inoperative due to improper input.
These issues can be avoided through properly designing
the Brownian circuit, as discussed later in Sec. III.
Each CJoin gate transition is bidirectional, consisting
of forward and backward directions. A forward transition
progresses the computation, representing the movement
from a pre-logic state to a post-logic state as part of a
logic mapping step. Although each gate may, in principle,
have its own transition rates, we assume throughout this
study that all gates share a common forward transition
rate and distinct backward transition rates.
To demonstrate circuit construction using CJoin gates,
we show a NAND logic gate implementation [25]. The
NAND circuit, shown in Fig. 2, embeds two logic vari-
ables, A and B in four wires: “A,” “not A,” “B” and
“not B.” The presence of a particle on a wire corresponds
to the logic state True, while its absence corresponds to
False. Each logic state is represented by a specific combi-
nation of two particles placed on four wires, and only four
out of the 4C2 = 6 possible configurations correspond to
valid logic configurations.
In Fig. 2(a), particles are initially set on the wires la-
beled “A” and “not B”, corresponding to the input state
A = True and B = False. This is a valid logic state from
which the corresponding gate operation can begin. This
approach resembles differential logic in electronic logic
circuits, though instead of comparing wire pairs, particles
exclusively ensure logical consistency during the circuit
operation.
Figs. 2(b) and (c) show how specific input
configurations activate particular gates. For instance, if
A = True and B = False, the second gate from the top
left becomes active. As Brownian particles move through
the circuit, they eventually reach the output wires, yield-
ing a logic output True for the NAND function, as shown
in Fig. 2(d).
The NAND function is implemented by
connecting the output wires as specified to produce the
corresponding logic output. Logical operations are ex-
pressed using standard Boolean notation: ”+” denotes
logical sum:OR, ”·” denotes logical product:AND, and
the negation operator “¯” generates the logical comple-
ment of a given logical variable. For example, ¯A denotes
the negation of A.
The concept of “simple rules and components” allows
various implementations of Brownian circuits. Although
our discussion focuses on the CJoin gate, other gate types
and design rules can also be employed.
Brownian cir-
cuits using CJoin gates are known to be computationally
universal[23, 24], and a previous study has established
mathematical equivalences among different gate types
and classified them[23]. In this paper, however, we re-
strict our discussion to circuits based on the CJoin gate
and concentrate on analyzing their dynamical behavior.

However, by restricting our focus on Brownian
circuits, we can interpret the components of computa-

Brownian circuits can be constructed using various gate
types and can be interpreted as Brownian motion on a
state-transition diagram, we consider the following three
aspects to provide both mathematical and physical de-
scriptions of computational cost:
a.
Computation time
We define the computation
time of a Brownian circuit in terms of the first passage
time (FPT) of Brownian particles. The FPT is the time
required for all particles in the circuit to move from their
initial positions to the completion of the computation. As
described in IV A, a Brownian circuit can be represented
as the motion of a single particle on a state-transition
diagram.
Thus, we consider the FPT of this effective
single-particle Brownian motion in this representation.
Since FPT is a probabilistic quantity, we define the com-
putation time as its mean value.
b.
Circuit size
We define circuit size as the total
number of gates. For example, if N modules, each con-
sisting of m gates, are connected in series, the total num-
ber of gates is mN, which measures the circuit’s overall
scale.
Since circuit size can vary significantly depend-
ing on the design, we introduce a practical circuit size in
Sec. III to reflect meaningful comparisons. This is anal-
ogous to circuit complexity in computational theory[26],
where Size(f) denotes the size of a circuit required to
implement the mapping f : {0, 1}N →{0, 1}. We denote
the set of functions that can be implemented with size
at most G(n) as the complexity class SIZE(G(n)). While
the inherently probabilistic nature of Brownian circuits
means that circuit complexity does not fully characterize
their behavior, it remains a useful measure of computa-
tional cost.
c.

that transitions in CJoin gates obey the local detailed-

guarantees that any given function can, in principle, be
realized using Brownian circuits.
While our numeri-
cal analysis later focuses mainly on adders, the design
method described here can be generally applied to any
logic function. In this section, we explain how to con-

A.
Circuit structure and representation
Since CJoin gates operate only when two particles si-
multaneously arrive at a gate, each transition updates
only a part of the overall system state, even if the global
state remains uniquely defined. As a result, some circuit
elements can operate independently in parallel; we refer
to these as parallel elements. Conversely, certain gates
must operate sequentially, after the input state is set, to
complete the computation. The longest such sequence of
dependent gates is called the critical path, and the num-
ber of gates along this path is denoted by Ng. In deter-
ministic circuits, such as conventional electronic circuits,
the total computational time is typically proportional to
the length of this critical path.
We construct CJoin-based circuits incrementally, be-
ginning with a two-particle input/output (I/O) circuit.
For two logical input variables and their corresponding
outputs, there are 24 × 24 = 256 possible configurations.

the four possible logic products: A·B, A· ¯B, ¯A·B, ¯A· ¯B. By
appropriately rearranging the connections between these
outputs, all sixteen possible two-variable logic functions
can be realized.

B.
Modular circuits; Adders
In this subsection, we present and compare three mod-
ular designs for implementing adders in Brownian cir-
cuits: the full adder, the precede adder, and the prod-
uct adder. While all three designs implement the same
logical function for binary addition, they differ in how
carry information propagates through the circuit, result-
ing in different critical path lengths. These differences are
not only structurally significant but also lead to marked
differences in computational time, particularly in how
it scales with the number of digits, as we will demon-
strate in the following section. We begin by introduc-
ing standard logic gate designs, including fundamental
components such as AND, OR, and other fundamental
gates.[20] Using the boxing representation described in
the previous subsection, their schematic structures can
be presented as follows.
The adder is one of the most fundamental components
in logic circuits. Adders perform integer addition and,
like other logic circuits, typically handle binary numbers.
Consider an adder module: each module takes two input
bits and produces both the sum and the carry-out bit.
A module without a carry-in bit is called a half adder,
while one that receives a carry-in bit is called a full adder.
By connecting multiple adder modules in series with the
carry-in and carry-out bits, we can construct a circuit
that enables multi-digit binary addition. In this study,
we employ a module design approach to control the over-
all circuit size by changing the number of modules. The
total circuit size is proportional to the number of mod-
ules, with the proportionality constant depending on the
circuit design used. Fig. 5 shows a conventional adder
circuit adapted for Brownian implementation. We refer
to this circuit as the full adder.

cuits, where the implementation of carry propagation sig-
nificantly affects performance, we introduce two alterna-
tive adder designs: the precede adder and the product
adder. While these designs realize the same logic func-

C.
Brownian circuits with sum-of-products normal
form
In the previous subsection, we presented several con-
crete modular designs of adders to demonstrate how the
specific logic functions can be implemented in Brown-
ian circuits. While those designs indicate the validity of
Brownian computation for specific tasks, it is also cru-
cial to understand how arbitrary logical mappings can be
systematically implemented.
Here, we examine the functional completeness of Brow-
nian circuits by demonstrating that any logic function
can be realized using the sum-of-product (SoP) normal
form.
The SoP form, also referred to as the disjunc-
tive normal form, is a standard representation in logic
design. By explicitly constructing an SoP-based circuit
using CJoin gates, we establish a universal construction
scheme and clarify how its circuit size and computational
cost scale with input size. Although it is well known that
CJoin gates form a universal gate set for Brownian cir-
cuits [23, 24], our focus here is to analyze this universality
from the perspective of scalability and efficiency.
In the SoP normal form, a logic function is expressed as
a logical sum of product terms of its logic input variables.
For example, the following function is written in SoP
form:
f(A, B, C) = A · B · C + ¯A · B · C + B · ¯C
Since any logic function can be transformed into this SoP
normal form, constructing a Brownian circuit for an arbi-
trary SoP function is sufficient to implement all possible
logic mappings. Below, we explicitly demonstrate how to
construct such circuits using CJoin gates.
Figure 8 shows an SoP circuit with four variables.
These inputs are processed sequentially, with each stage
keeping the accumulated logic information. At each state
n, the number of wires increases by 2n+1, reflecting all
possible combinations of the input variables up to that
point. The final stage, therefore, encodes the complete
n + 1 bit input information across the wires. This struc-
ture yields a one-dimensional state-transition diagram
with linear depth in the input size but an exponential
number of gates and wires. Specifically, an SoP circuit
with N inputs requires N + 1 stages and 2N wires to
represent all input combinations. The number of gates

IV.
STATE-TRANSITION DYNAMICS AND
FIRST PASSAGE TIME IN BROWNIAN
CIRCUITS
In Brownian circuits,
computation is carried out
through the stochastic motion of Brownian particles
along wires, with CJoin gates inducing transitions be-
tween logic states. Rather than explicitly tracking indi-
vidual particle trajectories, it is often more convenient
to describe the dynamics in terms of the circuit’s state-
transition diagram, a discrete graph whose nodes repre-
sent logic states and whose edges represent allowed tran-
sitions. In this representation, computation becomes a
stochastic process on a finite graph, and the first-passage
time (FPT) to the unique completed state provides a nat-
ural measure of computational time. To establish a the-
oretical basis for analyzing Brownian circuits, we first re-
call the classical FPT properties of one-dimensional drift-
diffusion processes and reinterpret them as a dynamical
phase transition that is directly relevant to computa-
tional behavior in Brownian circuits. This perspective
provides a reference point for evaluating the time com-
plexity of more general circuit architectures.
We then
introduce an embedding method that maps the dynam-
ics on an arbitrary state-transition graph onto an effec-
tive one-dimensional graph, clarifying how structural fea-
tures of the graph influence the balance between forward
and backward transitions.
Finally, we discuss general
structural properties of Brownian circuits and formulate
a conjecture on the existence of easy-hard transitions in
generic Brownian circuits.

A.
State-transition diagram of Brownian circuits
Since Brownian particles are classical, we treat each
one as localized at a discrete point along a wire. In prac-
tice, Brownian motion causes each particle to relax to an
equilibrium distribution on its wire, and it remains on
that wire until a CJoin gate transfers it to a neighboring
wire. When the transition rate of the gates is sufficiently
slow compared with the relaxation time of Brownian mo-
tion along the wire, it is reasonable to model the system
as a Markov process in which transitions occur between
wire-localized equilibrium states. In this coarse-grained
picture, the position of a particle is fully described by
the wire it occupies.
By assigning logical variables to
the wires, the circuit’s dynamics can be mapped onto a
discrete logical state space, and the resulting stochastic
evolution is governed by a master equation. This repre-
sentation naturally leads to a state-transition diagram,
where nodes represent discrete logic states determined
by particle configurations, and edges represent allowed
transitions induced by gates.
A Brownian circuit can be represented as a discrete
Brownian motion on a state-transition diagram.
By
adopting a coarse-grained representation of particle po-
sitions as the logical variables of the wires, the particle
arrangement on the wires directly corresponds to a set
of logic variables, thereby forming a circuit logic state.
Consequently, the Brownian time evolution of the circuit
is equivalent to the motion of a single particle on the
state-transition diagram, with the logic states serving as
discrete coordinates.
Brownian circuits exhibit several key structural prop-
erties in their state-transition diagrams. First, for any
connected diagram, there exists a unique output state
that represents the computation’s completion. Second,
all and only those input states that are converted to the
same output state belong to a single connected compo-
nent in the same diagram. These properties arise from
the mathematical structure of logic circuits: every input
deterministically maps to a unique output, though the
mapping need not be injective, meaning that multiple
input states may correspond to the same output. The
specific structure of a state-transition diagram depends
on the underlying circuit design, and different circuit ar-
chitectures implementing the same logic function may re-
sult in distinct diagrams. However, some design methods
yield diagrams with fixed structure patterns, even when
the logic mapping is not trivially understood.
In this study, we adopt a modular design approach, al-
lowing the circuit size to be systematically varied. Each
module is a functional unit with a fixed number of inputs
and outputs that performs a prescribed local operation.
By connecting identical modules in series, we construct
circuits that support multi-bit operations.
The corre-
sponding state-transition diagram of such a modular cir-
cuit has a fixed depth, defined as the number of gates a
particle transfers from any initial state to the final out-
put state, because all input states pass through the same
sequence of operations to reach the output state.

C.
Embedding method

Neverthe-
less, many circuits have structural features that enable
their dynamics to be coarse-grained into an effective one-

vation that the state-transition diagrams of Brownian cir-

can be naturally ordered by their graph-theoretic dis-
tance from the final state.

1. Label all states according to their distance from the
final state.
2. Group states with the same distance and sum
their master equations to obtain an effective one-
dimensional chain.

D.
Conjecture on general graph
In general, the state-transition diagrams of Brown-
ian circuits exhibit complex structures involving multi-
ple branches and merges.
Such complexity makes ex-
act coarse-graining into a simple one-dimensional Markov
process rarely possible. Nevertheless, Brownian circuits
obey structural constraints that allow us to formulate
general statements about the scaling behavior of their
FPT.
Although a full reduction to one dimension is not pos-
sible for arbitrary circuits, it is still feasible to obtain a
local one-dimensional approximation by grouping states
according to their distance from the completed state and
summing the master equations within each group. This
procedure yields an effective escape rate governing the
probability flow between adjacent groups. The ratio of
its forward and backward contributions can then be in-
terpreted analogously to the γ+/γ−ratio in the strictly
one-dimensional setting.
Because Brownian circuits implement deterministic
logical mappings, each input corresponds to a unique
output. As a result, multiple distinct initial states must
converge to the single completed state through interme-
diate transitions.
This structural feature implies that
each intermediate state typically has exactly one forward
transition but multiple backward transitions. The total
backward transition rate, therefore, tends to exceed the
forward transition rate. This does not mean that back-
ward transitions always dominate. Rather, it indicates a
generic structural bias toward backward motion, which
must be compensated for by a sufficiently large forward
rate to ensure efficient computation.
To quantify this imbalance, we introduce a factor a
representing the average number of backward transitions
per forward transition. This leads to a generalized bal-
ance condition for the easy-hard transition:
γ+ = aγ−.
(20)
In the purely one-dimensional case discussed in Sec. IV B,
we have a = 1, recovering the standard threshold γ+ =
γ−. In more complex circuits, where converging paths is
unavoidable and backward transitions are more frequent,
one expects, in general, a > 1. This consideration leads
to the following conjecture: for general Brownian cir-
cuits, the easy-hard transition occurs when the forward
rate compensates the backward bias characterized by a.
Equivalently, unless the state-transition graph effectively
reduces to a one-dimensional chain (a = 1), the transition
point shifts to γ+ = aγ0 with a > 1. An exception is the
SoP circuit discussed in III C, which effectively reduces
to a one-dimensional structure, and thus satisfies a = 1.
However, the consequences of this exception, including
the associated cost trade-offs, are discussed in Sec. VI.

A.
Sum-of-Product circuit: finite-size scaling and
phase transition
As discussed in Sec. III C, Brownian circuits can gen-

normal form with a one-dimensional state-transition di-

in one dimension exhibits a qualitative change in asymp-

a phase transition as a function of the gate transition
rate in the continuous spatial limit. To confirm this phe-
nomenon numerically, we perform stochastic simulations
of Brownian circuits on a one-dimensional discrete lattice
and examine how the FPT scales with circuit size.
We employ the Gillespie algorithm[29] to generate
stochastic trajectories of Brownian motion from input to
output, following the transition rules defined in Sec. IV D.
This approach provides empirical FPT distributions.

the backward transition rate at γ−= 1. The resulting

VI.
DISCUSSION AND SUMMARY
A.
Phase transition; balance of forward and
backward
In this study, we have identified a dynamical phase
transition in Brownian circuits that separates regimes of
linear and exponential scaling of the mean FPT with re-
spect to circuit size. Finite-size scaling analyses confirm
the existence of a critical forward transition rate, denoted
by γc, at which this change in scaling behavior occurs.
Throughout this work, γc refers to the critical value of
the forward rate γ+ at which the scaling of the mean FPT
changes, while the backward rate γ−is held fixed and sets
the unit of time in numerical evaluation. The associated
scaling exponent is found to be universal, α = 2, inde-
pendent of circuit details. This value agrees with the an-
alytical result in Eq. (12) for one-dimensional continuous
Brownian motion, indicating that the Brownian circuits
exhibiting such a transition share the same critical scal-
ing behavior as effective one-dimensional drift-diffusion
processes.

More generally, by applying the embedding method
(Sec. IV C), the dynamics of a Brownian circuit can be
reduced to an effective one-dimensional master equation
defined on the computational-state space. Within this
reduced description, the imbalance between forward and
backward transitions can be quantified explicitly. The
characteristic transition rate γc is then determined by
the condition under which the effective forward and back-
ward probability fluxes balance.
For the SoP circuit, the forward and backward transi-
tion rates at intermediate states are exactly symmetric,
leading to γc = γ−. In contrast, in modular circuits such
as adders, state-transition diagrams always contain merg-
ing points where multiple computational paths converge
to a single state. At such merging states, the number of
backward transitions exceeds that of forward transitions,
which shifts the balance condition and results in γc > γ−.
Since no practical circuit exhibits a strict one-to-one cor-
respondence between forward and backward transitions,
except for the idealized SoP construction, most Brown-
ian circuits are expected to satisfy γc > γ−.
We em-
phasize that this inequality should be understood as a
general conjecture rather than a strict theorem. This re-
flects a structural bias toward backward transitions that
arises universally in Brownian circuits that implement
non-trivial computations.

performance of the circuit. Meanwhile, circuit complex-
ity, as measured by circuit size, also constrains the over-
all computational time. Consequently, the computation
cost of a Brownian circuit must be understood in terms
of both energy input and circuit size.
For SoP circuits, the state-transition diagram is effec-
tively one-dimensional, and the forward/backward bal-
ance is exactly symmetric at every intermediate state.
Consequently, the dynamical transition occurs at γc =
γ−, corresponding to the zero-energy limit.
At first
glance, this suggests an energetic advantage. However,
this benefit is compensated by the exponential growth of
circuit size, such that a function with N inputs requires
O
 2N
gates and wires. Although the computation time
remains polynomial for γ+ ≥γ−, linear for γ+ > γ−,
and quadratic at γ+ = γ−, the exponential growth in cir-
cuit size dominates the total computational cost. Thus,
even though zero-energy computation is formally realized
at γ+ = γ−, SoP circuits are not computationally effi-
cient because their space complexity becomes consider-
ably more cumbersome than any advantages gained from
the energy-balanced design.
In contrast, modular circuits realize arithmetic opera-
tions with circuit size growing only linearly in the input
size N.
However, their state-transition diagrams con-
tain merging points where multiple computational paths
converge, leading to an excess of backward transitions
and therefore a critical rate satisfying γc > γ−.
Our
numerical study of three types of N-digit adders, the
Full Adder, the Product Adder, and the Precede Adder,
quantitatively demonstrates these characteristics.
The Precede Adder exhibits no phase transition in its
FPT scaling and shows exponential growth of the mean
FPT for all values of γ+, indicating that its effective tran-
sition graph suppresses forward propagation too strongly.
In contrast, both the Full Adder and the Product Adder
exhibit clear transition points at which the scaling of the
mean FPT changes from linear to exponential in N. The
transition points are numerically estimated as γc = 1.43
for the Full Adder and γc = 2.34 for the Product Adder,
with γ−= 1 setting the unit of time. Through the local
detailed balance condition in Eq. (22), these values corre-
spond to finite energy thresholds, explicitly demonstrat-
ing that efficient computation requires a nonzero energy
input.
Although the adders examined here represent only spe-
cific examples of modular circuits, their state-transition
diagrams share a structural motif: repeated merging of
computational pathways.
This structure generally in-
creases the total backward transition rate and therefore
raises γc above unity. We therefore expect the qualita-
tive scaling behavior observed here to hold broadly across
modular circuit architectures.
These observations reveal a fundamental trade-off.
SoP circuits achieve γc = γ−but require exponential
space, whereas modular circuits achieve linear space but
necessarily satisfy γc > γ−.
Consequently, no Brow-
nian circuit simultaneously achieves polynomial circuit
size and energy-free computation time. More precisely,
for a modular circuit of size Θ(N), efficient computation
in the sense of polynomial mean FPT requires
γ+ ≥γc > γ−
(23)
At the transition point γ+ = γc, the scaling becomes
quadratic in the input size, ⟨τ⟩= Θ(N 2), while for
γ+ < γc the mean FPT grows exponentially.
There-
fore, energy-free computation (γ+ = γ−) is possible only
in circuits whose size already scales exponentially. For
Brownian circuits with polynomial complexity, a strictly

In the computationally efficient regime, the computa-

Here, we

not only by short mean computation times but also by
high temporal reliability. While such vanishing relative
fluctuations are consistent with the properties of one-
dimensional drift-diffusion processes, as discussed in Ap-

observed at the transition point. This finding suggests
that the easy-hard transition is accompanied by a simul-

tation time and its statistical variability.
This viewpoint provides an alternative characteriza-
tion of computational cost in Brownian circuits. In con-
trast to the conventional approach of allowing arbitrar-
ily long computation times, this study employs scaling
laws based on computational size to evaluate computa-
tional efficiency.
This approach follows the concept of
computational complexity theory. From this perspective,
Brownian circuits exhibit a clear and unavoidable trade-
off between circuit size and the energy input required for
efficient computation.
C.
Design: Serial or Parallel
Circuits operate according to the order in which infor-
mation propagates; in Brownian circuits, this ordering
is determined by the sequence of the stochastic parti-
cle’s motion.
Modular circuits, whose components are
connected in series, generally operate sequentially. How-
ever, depending on the circuit structure, there may exist
paths along which information propagates independently,
allowing certain circuit elements to operate in parallel.
In deterministic electronic circuits, such parallelism typi-
cally reduces computation time. The behavior of parallel
elements in Brownian circuits, however, is fundamentally
different and is analyzed in the Appendix D, where fully
parallel architectures are discussed explicitly.
The distinction between serial and parallel architec-
tures significantly influences the scaling of computa-
tional cost. A completely serial design, such as a one-
dimensional chain or the SoP circuit, exhibits the same
scaling behavior discussed earlier: linear scaling of the
mean FPT for γ+ > γ−, quadratic scaling at γ+ = γ−,
and exponential divergence for γ+ < γ−.
In contrast,
a completely parallel design, in which all modules oper-
ate independently, generates a state-transition diagram
equivalent to a hypercube. For such circuits, the mean
FPT grows exponentially with input size for all val-
ues of γ+, indicating that no parameter regime yields
polynomial-time computation.
This contrast indicates a fundamental design principle
for Brownian circuits. In deterministic electronic circuits,
adding parallel components generally reduces computa-
tion time because multiple logic elements operate simul-
taneously, shortening the critical path. Brownian circuits
behave quite differently: because forward progress is gov-
erned by probabilistic transitions, simultaneous forward
moves across many parallel components are extremely
rare events. As a result, highly parallel architectures lead
to longer computation times, with FPT scaling exponen-
tially even when the energy input is large.
These considerations imply that efficient Brownian cir-
cuits require a serial organization of computational mod-
ules.
Parallel elements should be minimized or, when
unavoidable, constrained so that their influence does not
accumulate across multiple stages of the computation.
Thus, conventional circuit-design strategies developed for
deterministic electronic systems cannot be directly ap-
plied to Brownian circuits.
Instead, Brownian circuits
must be designed with their probabilistic dynamics, en-
suring that the effective state-transition structure re-
mains close to one-dimensional to maintain polynomial-
time computation.
D.
Critical points and general implications
Our analysis shows that the existence of a dynam-
ical phase transition in the FPT of a Brownian cir-
cuit is determined by the balance between forward

16
and backward transition rates.
Circuits whose state-
transition diagrams can be effectively approximated by
one-dimensional chains exhibit a clear transition at
a characteristic rate γc, whereas circuits with strong
branching or extensive parallelism may exhibit no such
phase transition. Determining whether a given circuit ex-
hibits a phase transition, therefore, requires understand-
ing how its state-transition diagram can be reduced to an
effective one-dimensional stochastic process and whether
the forward and backward probability fluxes can balance
at any point.
In simple structural diagrams, such as those discussed
in Appendix E, this balance can be calculated explic-
itly because the equilibrium distribution is analytically
tractable. However, for general Brownian circuits, the
state-transition diagrams grow rapidly in both size and
structural complexity. This makes it difficult, in practice,
to compute or even sample the equilibrium distribution
required to determine γc. As a result, characterizing the
existence and location of a critical point remains nontriv-
ial for large or irregular circuit architectures.
Nevertheless, the conceptual framework developed in
this study applies not only to Brownian circuits com-
posed of CJoin gates but also to a more general class of
Brownian computation systems driven by Brownian mo-
tion, whose logical gates are encoded by the positions of
Brownian particles. In such systems, the computational
process can generally be interpreted as the stochastic mo-
tion of a particle on a discrete state-transition diagram.
Whenever the effective topology of this diagram is suffi-
ciently close to one dimension, a similar easy-hard phase
transition in computational time is expected to occur.
Finally, the phase-transition picture developed in this
work is not limited to artificial Brownian circuits. Re-
lated phenomena are likely to occur in real-world systems
that rely on Brownian motion for information processing
or sequential control. A notable example is DNA tran-
scription, which proceeds as an essentially serial Brown-
ian process along a one-dimensional template. This ar-
chitecture may be interpreted as a design choice avoiding
the drastic slowdown associated with excessive branching
and parallelism. Future research into these connections
could yield significant insights, particularly by identify-
ing biological and soft-matter systems that either exploit
or avoid easy-hard transitions. In this broader context,
our results suggest that maintaining fast and reliable
Brownian computation generally requires a finite ener-
getic bias, making energy input an inherent component
of fluctuation-driven information processing.

Appendix D: Complete binary tree and fully parallel
circuits
In this appendix, we present two examples that clarify
how the structure of a state-transition diagram influences
the scaling behavior of the FPT in Brownian circuits.
The first example is a complete binary tree, which repre-
sents the simplest case in which multiple backward tran-
sitions occur at every intermediate state. As discussed in
Sec. IV D, an m-ary branching structure is expected to
exhibit an easy-hard transition at the generalized balance
condition γ+ = mγ−; therefore, the complete binary tree
(m = 2) provides a minimal nontrivial test case for the
predicted critical rate γc = 2γ−.
A Brownian circuit whose state-transition diagram
forms a complete binary tree is, under the embedding
method, equivalent to a one-dimensional diagram in
which the backward transition rate is twice the forward
rate. Therefore, the predicted critical point for scaling
switching is γc = 2γ−. Figire 14 presents numerical re-
sults for the mean FPT and its finite-size scaling. The
result clearly exhibits a dynamical phase transition at
γc = 2γ−, fully consistent with the theoretical prediction
for a binary-branching structure.
This provides direct
numerical evidence supporting the conjecture proposed
in Sec. IV D.
To provide a complementary example, we examine a
fully parallel circuit in which all modules operate inde-
pendently, and each module consists of a single gate.
The circuit accepts an input only when all modules
have reached their final state. The corresponding state-
transition graph is an N dimensional hypercube, as dis-
cussed in Sec. IV C. In this architecture, progress in com-
putation requires simultaneous forward transitions across
multiple independent components. Consequently, there
is no finite value of γ+ for which the FPT becomes poly-
nomial in N.
In other words, the system exhibits no
easy-hard transition, corresponding formally to γc →∞.
