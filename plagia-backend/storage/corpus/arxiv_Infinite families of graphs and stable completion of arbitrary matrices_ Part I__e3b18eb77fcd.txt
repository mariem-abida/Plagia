Abstract
We study deterministic constructions of graphs for which the unique completion of
low rank matrices is generically possible regardless of the values of the entries. We
relate the completability to the presence of some patterns (particular unions of self-
avoiding walks) in the subgraph of the lattice graph generated from the support of the
bi-adjacency matrix. The construction makes it possible to design infinite families of
graphs on which exact and stable completion is possible for every fixed rank matrix
through the sum-of-squares hierarchy.

Introduction
Given a matrix X0 ∈M(m×n, r) ⊂Rm×n, where M(m×n; r) denotes the manifold of rank-r
matrices, the completion problem consists in recovering X0 given a subset Ω⊂E = [m]×[n]
of its entries. The known and missing entries of the matrix are usually represented through
the bipartite graph G = (V, W, E) with V = [m], W = [n] corresponding to the row and
column indices of X0. In its general form, the low rank matrix completion problem finds
numerous applications from collaborative filtering [1] to vision and control [2], including
genomic data integration [3].
In the particular case of Gram (i.e. positive semidefinite matrices) of the form X = PP ⊺, the
columns of P can be regarded as points in Rr and the matrix X can be regarded as specifying
a subset of pairwise distances between points in Rr. The problem then consists in recovering
the positions of all the points. Applications of this geometric formulation sometimes known as
graph realization can be found in sensor localization [4, 5], structural biology (i.e. molecular
conformation, NMR spectroscopy and the molecule problem) [6].

3.2
Deterministic/algebraic constructions
As explained above, despite clear theoretical interests (in particular when combined with
convex optimization) the incoherence assumptions and/or γ2-norm do not provide intuition
on what “drives” the completability of low rank matrices. I.e. what makes the difference
between the patterns that can be completed and those that cannot, and among the patterns
that can be completed in theory, the difference between those that can be completed effi-
ciently and those for which the exact completion, while possible in theory, remains out of
reach in practice. Around Theorem 1, a number of approaches have attempted to character-
ize successful sampling patterns per se, that is to say with respect to the structure itself and
without the need for additional assumptions on the unknown matrix. Those approaches can
roughly be organized into two groups: the first group (which includes [17]) relies on combi-
natorics and matroid theory, notions from algebraic geometry such as the characterization of
the determinantal variety and the Jacobian of the map A : U, V 7→A = UV ⊺and/or the
Grassmanian. The second relies on rigidity theory and the distance geometry problem (a.k.a.
graph realization problem).
Both approaches usually make the distinction between finite completability (or local com-
pletability in rigidity theory) and unique completability (or global completability in rigidity
theory). The terms finite or unique completability are used to denote settings in which com-
pleting the matrix uniquely given the pattern is not possible but the number of completion
remains finite. Conversely the authors use the terms global or unique completability when
the low rank completion is guaranteed to be unique. The classical references along those two
directions can be summarized as follows.
