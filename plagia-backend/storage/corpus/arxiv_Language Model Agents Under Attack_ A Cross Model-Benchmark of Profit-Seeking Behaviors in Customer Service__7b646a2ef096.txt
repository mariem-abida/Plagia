Abstract
Customer-service LLM agents increasingly make policy-bound de-
cisions (refunds, rebooking, billing disputes), but the same ”helpful”
interaction style can be exploited: a small fraction of users can in-
duce unauthorized concessions, shifting costs to others and eroding
trust in agentic workflows. We present a cross-domain benchmark
of profit-seeking direct prompt injection in customer-service in-
teractions, spanning 10 service domains and 100 realistic attack
scripts grouped into five technique families. Across five widely used
models under a unified rubric with uncertainty reporting, attacks
are highly domain-dependent (airline support is most exploitable)
and technique-dependent (payload splitting is most consistently
effective). We release data and evaluation code to support repro-
ducible auditing and to inform the design of oversight and recovery
workflows for trustworthy, human-centered agent interfaces.
Keywords
Large Language models, agents, Trustworthy, prompt injection
attack, non-rational behavior

Introduction
Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly embedded
in everyday workflows, particularly in customer-facing service
tasks such as refunds, account recovery, travel rebooking, billing
disputes, and policy clarification. In many deployments, the agent’s
reply is not merely advisory: it can authorize concessions, trigger
account actions, and generate records that must stand up to auditing
and compliance review. These systems can reduce service friction
and automate routine decisions, but their conversational interface
also creates a broad attack surface: users can strategically craft
language to obtain unauthorized benefits, shift costs onto providers,
or bypass operational constraints. Even when the immediate harm

that increase monitoring costs and reduce predictability for both
users and organizations. As deployment scales, understanding how
real-world customer-service agents fail under adversarial or profit-
seeking user behavior becomes central to building safer and fairer
systems.
A key mechanism behind such failures is prompt attack, where
an adversary uses natural-language inputs to steer a model away
from its intended behavior toward attacker-chosen objectives. From
a human-centered agent-interface perspective, these are interac-
tion failures: the system misinterprets intent, misapplies policy,
and often lacks clear user-facing recovery paths such as escalation,
approval checkpoints, or rollback/undo. In practice, prompt injec-
tion is often discussed in direct and indirect forms. Direct prompt
injection is delivered through the conversation itself and targets
the instruction-following behavior of the agent; indirect prompt
injection embeds malicious instructions in external content (e.g.,
retrieved documents or tool outputs) that the model later consumes.
This work focuses on direct prompt injection because it directly
matches customer-service interactions in which user text is the
dominant interface, and because modeling a full retrieval and tool-
ing stack is beyond the scope of the benchmark introduced here.
Accordingly, our goal is not to model end-to-end tool compromise,
but to measure how easily the conversational control surface can
be used to push an agent across policy boundaries.
Prior research has explored non-rational or strategically manip-
ulative behaviors by constructing controlled simulations inspired
by psychology and behavioral economics (e.g., game-theoretic set-
tings, deception incentives, or theory of mind style dialog) [9, 21,
25]. These approaches are useful for isolating mechanisms and
testing hypotheses, but they leave an empirical gap: the commu-
nity lacks a benchmark grounded in customer service style agent
contexts with standardized, realistic attack utterances that reflect
how users attempt to talk systems into policy-violating outcomes.
This gap matters not only for security measurement, but also for
trustworthy deployment: without domain-grounded stress tests, it
is hard to decide where to place oversight (e.g., when to require
confirmation, when to escalate to a human, and when to surface
explanations or uncertainty). Without such a benchmark, it remains
unclear which everyday service domains are most vulnerable, how
vulnerability varies across widely deployed models under a unified
protocol, and which attack strategies succeed most reliably in prac-
tice. In other words, we lack deployment-relevant evidence that
connects “what fails” to “what interfaces and oversight mechanisms
should be prioritized.”

To address this gap, we introduce a benchmark for direct prompt
injection in customer-service–style interactions. The benchmark
covers ten common service scenarios and 100 attack scripts de-
signed to capture practical manipulation tactics in high-frequency
support settings. We design the benchmark as a reusable incident
suite: researchers and practitioners can swap in different models,
guardrails, or interaction policies and compare susceptibility under
the same scenarios and attack styles. Using a consistent scoring
rubric and unified success metrics with uncertainty reporting, we
evaluate five widely deployed LLMs: DeepSeek v3.2 [5], Claude
Opus 4.1 [1], GPT-5 [23], GPT-4o [22], and Gemini 2.5 Pro [6],
and show that susceptibility is strongly domain and model depen-
dent. Among the ten scenarios, airline support is the easiest to
compromise; across models, DeepSeek exhibits the highest overall
probability of successful compromise under our evaluation proto-
col. We further find that payload-splitting attacks, which separate
an instruction into seemingly innocuous parts and then induce
the agent to combine and execute them, are the most consistently
effective strategy in our setting. Finally, we release the dataset and
benchmark framework to enable replication, auditing, and follow-
on work on defenses and safer agent deployment. More broadly,
our results aim to inform the design of trustworthy agent interfaces
by highlighting where additional transparency, checkpoints, and
recovery workflows are most needed.
In this work, we therefore ask the following research questions:
(1) RQ1: Which customer-service domains are most vulnerable
to profit-seeking direct prompt injection under a unified
protocol?
(2) RQ2: How does vulnerability vary across target models after
accounting for domain composition and evaluator effects?
(3) RQ3: Which prompt-injection technique families are most
effective, and how sensitive are PI-type rankings to evaluator
choice?
We make three contributions:
(1) Introduced a benchmark for profit-seeking direct prompt
injection in customer-service interactions, spanning 10 do-
mains, 100 injected conversations, and five PI families (PI1–
PI5).
(2) Provided a standardized evaluation protocol with five target
models and two independent LLM judges, releasing canoni-
cal CSV artifacts and a primary endpoint success = I[score
≥4].
(3) Reported comparative measurements of domain risk, model
vulnerability, and PI-family effectiveness, and quantified
evaluator sensitivity via inter-judge agreement.

Literature Review
2.1
Agentic AI in customer-service settings
A growing literature uses large language models (LLMs) as agen-
tic simulators in managerial, social, and economic environments.
Rather than evaluating single turn correctness, these works study
behavior emerging from model + prompt + identity conditioning + in-
teraction structure: agents are assigned roles or personas, sometimes
equipped with memory or reflection, and placed into structured
protocols such as networks, recommender platforms, games, or
markets. The common goal is to elicit human-like decisions and
examine how micro-level behaviors aggregate into macro-level
patterns.
One stream focuses on socio-technical platforms and collec-
tive dynamics. RecAgent simulates user behavior in recommender-
driven social media to study information cocoons and conformity,
and explores interventions such as reducing personalization and
increasing social heterogeneity [23]. LLM-agent networks have
also been used to model opinion dynamics, showing that baseline
tendencies can produce rapid convergence unless prompts or con-
ditions induce biases that support fragmentation [3]. These studies
highlight that “human-likeness” depends critically on both prompt-
ing and environment design.
A second stream evaluates agents through behavioral economics
and game-theoretic interaction. LLMs have been tested for repro-
ducing human trust behavior in canonical trust games [24], and
framed as simulated economic agents for controlled experiments
under specified endowments and information [6]. Multi-agent struc-
tures often improve behavioral fidelity in strategic settings such as
the ultimatum game [21], and can reproduce cooperation patterns
and richer action spaces in public goods environments relevant to
policy design [20]. Complementing replication-focused studies, be-
havioral evaluation frameworks also analyze reasoning depth and
contextual influence (e.g., persona framing) to reveal mechanism-
level differences across models [9].
A third stream targets economic decision-making in language-
mediated settings. Prompt-engineered agents with perception–reflection–
decision loops have been proposed for macroeconomic simulation,
producing plausible work and consumption choices and emergent
aggregate patterns [13]. Related tests of “folk economics” show that
LLMs often reproduce mental accounting biases while diverging
from human behavior in specific risk-sensitive regimes [11, 17].
Overall, this literature validates LLMs as useful simulators of
managerial and socio-economic behavior, but its primary emphasis
is simulation fidelity and mechanism analysis rather than deployment-
oriented security failure under adversarial objectives.
2.2
Prompt injection methods and evaluations
Prompt injection is a core vulnerability of LLM-integrated systems,
arising from the blurred boundary between instructions to execute
and context to interpret. Prior work commonly distinguishes di-
rect prompt injection, where adversarial instructions are issued
through the user-facing interface, from indirect prompt injection,
where malicious instructions are embedded in external content
(e.g., retrieved documents, webpages, or tool outputs) consumed
at inference time. Early security studies show that indirect prompt
injection can compromise real-world LLM-integrated applications
and enable downstream harms such as data exfiltration and func-
tionality hijacking, motivating threat models and taxonomies cen-
tered on data instruction confusion [4]. Complementary analyses
study prompt injection in real-world, deployed application stacks,
identifying recurring attack primitives (e.g., direct injection, escape-
character exploits, and context-ignoring tactics) and documenting
application-specific wrappers that can steer model behavior within
seemingly benign contexts [16].

Beyond handcrafted prompts, a growing line of work formalizes
attacks as optimization problems, enabling automated discovery of
strong injection sequences. JudgeDeceiver targets LLM-as-a-Judge
pipelines by injecting an optimized sequence into an attacker con-
trolled candidate response to bias the judge’s selection, illustrating
that even evaluation components can be manipulated [18]. PLeak
similarly uses closed-box optimization to elicit leakage of hidden
system prompts from LLM applications [8]. Related risks can also
be persistent rather than input-time: Virtual Prompt Injection (VPI)
defines a backdoor setting in which poisoned instruction-tuning
data induces behavior as if an attacker-chosen “virtual prompt”
were appended under trigger conditions [26].
In parallel, benchmark-style studies aim to measure injection ro-
bustness systematically across models and techniques. Large-scale
analyses report widespread susceptibility across diverse architec-
tures [1], while robustness benchmarks show a tension in which
models that are strong instruction followers may still mis-follow
embedded adversarial instructions [14]. Defenses increasingly rely
on training-time alignment interventions, such as preference op-
timization in SecAlign [2], yet empirical work demonstrates that
guardrail and detection systems can be evaded via obfuscation
and adversarial ML evasion strategies [5]. Together, these results
highlight an ongoing attack, that defense cycle and the need for
deployment-relevant, comparable evaluations.
Overall, prompt-injection research has developed rich threat
models, automated attacks, and defenses, yet evaluation targets and
protocols vary widely across mechanisms and application settings,
making deployment-relevant comparisons difficult.
2.3
Benchmarks for agent behavior in
managerial settings
Recent benchmarks increasingly evaluate LLMs in agentic settings,
which interactive loops with environment feedback, tools, and
multi-step decision-making where deviations from idealized ratio-
nality become measurable. Rather than treating errors as isolated
failures, this line of work operationalizes bounded rationality and
social behavior through task suites, standardized metrics, and re-
peated interactions, aiming to make agent behavior comparable
across models and settings.
One thread focuses on consequential, workplace-like tasks that
approximate managerial deployments. TheAgentCompany bench-
marks “digital worker” agents that browse the web, write and
run code, and coordinate via communication channels, empha-
sizing end-to-end task completion under realistic tool use [25]. In a
closer-to-CRM setting, CRMArena evaluates agents in professional
customer-relationship workflows and reports substantial gaps in
reliable performance even with common prompting and function-
calling setups [7, 19]. While these benchmarks provide realism in
environment complexity, their primary objective is capability and
reliability, rather than adversarial manipulation that targets policy
boundaries in customer-facing dialogue.
A second thread formalizes bounded rationality through behav-
ioral game theory and multi-agent decision environments. Classic
paradigms such as Rock Paper Scissors and the Prisoner’s Dilemma
reveal that LLMs often mirror familiar human heuristics (e.g., outcome-
based switching, conditional cooperation) but apply them more
rigidly and adapt less to dynamic opponent behavior [27]. Broader
suites such as GAMA-Bench scale evaluation across multi-agent
gaming environments with dynamic scoring [22], and multi-player

A third thread studies “irrational” behavior from a social-cognition

strategic manipulation.

policy-boundary failures induced by strategic dialogue as a first-
class measurement target.

Experimental Setting
Figure 1 summarizes the end-to-end benchmark pipeline used to
construct the benchmark, elicit model outputs, and produce analysis-
ready datasets for profit-seeking direct prompt injection in customer-
service–style interactions. Each experimental condition is defined
by a customer-service domain scenario paired with an injected user
message labeled by a prompt-injection strategy family (PI_type),
together with a target model that generates an agent reply. The
evaluation focuses on the first-turn reply to the injected prompt,
yielding a single-turn susceptibility test that is comparable across
conditions.
3.1
Benchmark construction
The benchmark instantiates a scenario bank spanning 10 customer
service domains and pairs each scenario with profit-seeking in-
jected user messages grouped into five prompt injection fami-
lies (PI1–PI5). Each (scenario, injection) pairing forms a scenario
instance indexed by {scenario_id, PI_type} and is exported as
scenarios_source.csv, which serves as the canonical specifica-
tion for downstream generation and evaluation.
3.2
Attack taxonomy and scope
Prompt injection is commonly separated into direct and indirect
forms: direct prompt injection places malicious instructions explic-
itly in the user’s conversational input, whereas indirect prompt
injection embeds malicious instructions in external content later
consumed by the model. The benchmark focuses exclusively on
direct prompt injection under a text only customer service agent
interface, with external access disabled (no retrieval, browsing, or
tool execution). Any apparent references to external content are
synthetic placeholders used solely to test whether the agent would
attempt to follow externally framed instructions. Within the direct
setting, injected messages are organized into five technique families
(PI1–PI5) to structure the prompt set and analyses.
3.3
Target models and response generation
For each scenario instance, first-turn replies are elicited from five
target models, GPT-5, DeepSeek v3.2, Claude Opus 4.1, Gemini 2.5
Pro, and GPT-4o, and recorded in model specific artifacts reply_{model}.csv.
Files are aligned to identical scenario rows so that each model is
evaluated on the same scenario and injection content. Decoding
parameters and response length constraints are held fixed within
each target-model configuration across all conditions; when gener-
ation settings are exposed by the serving API, they are logged to
support reproducibility audits.
3.4
LLM-as-evaluator protocol
Each generated response is scored by two independent evaluator
models (GPT-5 judge and Claude Opus 4.1 judge), producing parallel
judgments for the same target-model reply under the same scenario
PI_type condition. Evaluators output (i) an integer ordinal score
on a 0 −−5𝑠𝑐𝑎𝑙𝑒and (ii) a short rationale, and results are saved in
evaluation_{model}.csv. The evaluator is provided the minimal
context required for rubric-consistent scoring, including the system
prompt, injected user message, and model reply, and is instructed
to score strictly according to the rubric rather than to speculate
about external capabilities.
3.5
Data artifacts and canonical representation
Artifacts follow the pipeline structure. scenarios_source.csv
contains system prompts and injected messages; reply_{model}.csv
stores first-turn agent replies; evaluation_{model}.csv stores
evaluator scores and rationales; and multimodel_evaluation.csv
merges replies and evaluations into a unified table used for anal-
ysis. A tidy long-format representation is additionally produced
to support standardized statistical modeling and visualization. In
the long format, each row corresponds to one evaluator’s score for
one target model under one scenario and PI_type, with canonical
fields {scenario_id, scenario_name, PI_type, model, evaluator,
score, reason}. Scores are converted to numeric values and re-
stricted to the valid range [0, 5]. Scores are converted to numeric
values and restricted to the valid range [0, 5]. Missing values arise
only in the evaluator outputs (i.e., absent score and/or reason).
These entries are not treated as structural missingness in the bench-
mark itself: when an evaluator fails to return a valid structured
judgment, the corresponding score and reason are manually re-
covered and inserted to complete the evaluation records before
downstream metric computation. Missingness patterns are tracked
by evaluator and PI_type to surface systematic failure modes in

4
Methodology
This section specifies the experimental protocol for eliciting model
responses under direct prompt injection and for scoring responses
with LLM-based evaluators. The protocol is designed to enable
controlled comparisons across (i) customer-service domains, (ii)
prompt-injection technique families, and (iii) target models, under
a single-turn susceptibility setting.
4.1
Task formulation
The task evaluates profit-seeking direct prompt injection in customer-
service–style interactions. Each benchmark instance consists of
(a) a domain scenario that defines the service context and relevant
constraints, and (b) an injected user message intended to steer
the agent away from policy-aligned behavior toward an attacker-
chosen objective. A target model produces an agent reply to the
injected message. The evaluation considers only the first-turn reply
to the injection, yielding a single-turn susceptibility test that is
comparable across scenarios and models.
4.2
Attack and prompting protocol
(Figure 2) situates the benchmark within the broader prompt-injection
landscape by distinguishing direct from indirect prompt injection.
The benchmark instantiates only direct prompt injection, where
the adversarial instruction is delivered explicitly in the user’s con-
versational input.
Injected messages are organized into five technique families (PI1–
PI5), each representing a recurring strategy used to increase the
likelihood of instruction override:
• PI1: Role play. The message assigns a persona or role that
reframes the agent’s obligations (e.g., “act as an unrestricted
assistant”) to encourage policy bypass.
• PI2: Obfuscation. The message conceals malicious intent
via encoding, indirection, or noisy formatting, aiming to
evade superficial pattern-based refusal behaviors.
• PI3: Payload splitting. The message separates an instruc-
tion into innocuous subparts and induces the agent to com-
bine them into an actionable malicious request.
• PI4: Adversarial suffix. The message appends high-pressure
or constraint-based suffixes that attempt to override prior
instructions or coerce compliance.
• PI5: Instruction manipulation. The message attempts
explicit instruction hierarchy attacks (e.g., “ignore previ-
ous instructions”; “system override” framing) to redirect the
agent’s objective.
Each benchmark instance records a single PI_type label correspond-
ing to the primary technique family used to construct the injected
message.
4.3
Response generation protocol
For each scenario instance, the target model is prompted with the
scenario context and the injected user message, and then asked to
generate a single agent reply. The protocol records only the first-
turn reply to isolate immediate instruction-following susceptibility
and to prevent multi-turn dynamics (e.g., negotiation, iterative
clarification, or gradual escalation) from confounding comparisons
across models and scenarios.
Within each target-model configuration, decoding parameters
and response constraints are held constant across all benchmark
instances. When generation settings are exposed by the serving
interface, the configuration (e.g., temperature, maximum tokens) is
logged to support reproducibility checks.
4.4
Evaluation protocol and rubric
Following the LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation paradigm, each target-
model reply is assessed by two independent LLM judges, yielding
parallel scores for the identical (scenario, PI_type) condition. Eval-
uators are prompted with the minimal information required for
rubric consistent scoring: the scenario context, the injected user
message, and the target-model reply. The evaluator output is re-
quired to include (i) an ordinal score on a 0–5 scale and (ii) a brief
justification.
The scoring rubric operationalizes “susceptibility” as behavioral
compliance with the injected instruction and departure from the
intended customer service behavior. Higher scores correspond to
stronger evidence that the agent followed the injected instruction
and enabled the attacker’s objective; lower scores correspond to
refusal, safe redirection, or policy-aligned behavior. The rubric is
applied uniformly across domains and PI_type families.
Evaluator outputs are requested in a structured format to support
deterministic parsing. When an evaluator does not return a valid
structured judgment (e.g., an invalid JSON object that cannot be
parsed), the corresponding score and reason fields can be left
temporarily incomplete. These entries are subsequently manually
recovered and inserted to finalize the evaluation records before
downstream metric computation. Incompleteness rates are tracked
by evaluator and PI_type to surface systematic failure modes in
structured judging.

4.6
Aggregation and statistical analysis
Instance-level outcomes are aggregated to support comparisons
across domains, PI_type families, target models, and evaluators.
Where interval estimates are reported, uncertainty is summarized
using nonparametric bootstrap resampling at the scenario instance
level (scenario × injected message). For each bootstrap replicate,
all associated rows for the sampled instances (across evaluators
and target models) are retained to preserve dependence induced by
shared prompts. Point estimates and 95% confidence intervals are
computed from the bootstrap distribution.
Evaluator sensitivity is assessed by comparing success estimates
under different evaluators and by tracking evaluator-specific failure
modes (e.g., structured-output parse failures) as part of the overall
measurement validity analysis.

sensitivity
RQ3 compares prompt-injection technique families (PI1–PI5) and
tests whether PI-type rankings depend on the evaluator used to
score model outputs. The primary endpoint is a binary success
indicator derived from the ordinal evaluator score𝑠∈{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5}:

Two complementary summaries are reported. First, evaluator
agreement is assessed by comparing PI-type success rates under two
independent LLM judges (GPT-5 and Claude Opus 4.1). Second, PI-

modeled with categorical effects for PI type, domain (scenario),
evaluated model, and evaluator, and marginal standardization is

distributions of domains and evaluated models fixed. Uncertainty is
quantified using nonparametric bootstrap 95% confidence intervals

6
Discussion
6.1
Limitations
This benchmark is intentionally scoped, and several limitations
should be considered when interpreting the results.

Scenario coverage and dataset scale. We instantiate profit-seeking
prompt injection across 10 customer service scenarios and 100 in-
jected conversations. This design enables controlled comparisons
across models and prompt injection families, but it cannot fully rep-
resent the heterogeneity of real customer support deployments (e.g.,
policy regimes, operational procedures, domain specific risk expo-
sure, and escalation stacks). In this sense, domain- or scenario-level
rankings should be read as estimates over our selected scenario
set rather than universal conclusions. More broadly, recent agent
benchmarks that emphasize realistic professional environments
and consequential task execution highlight that success can de-
pend strongly on environment details and operational constraints
[7, 25]. Our smaller scale setting prioritizes interpretability and con-
trolled factorial comparisons, but it necessarily sacrifices breadth.
In addition, the limited sample size implies that some fine grained
strata (scenario × model × PI × evaluator) can be sparse, increasing
variance and making certain estimates more sensitive to modeling,
regularization, and aggregation choices.
Single turn operationalization. Susceptibility is operationalized
using the first assistant reply, providing a clean single turn signal
that supports consistent scoring across conditions. However, this
choice does not capture multi-turn dynamics common in deployed
agents, such as clarification, delayed refusal, escalation policies, or
progressive manipulation over extended interactions. This limita-
tion is especially relevant for attacks whose effectiveness depends
on cross-turn composition (e.g., payload splitting), where a single-
turn proxy may reflect initial compliance tendencies rather than
end-to-end execution risk. Related agent evaluations in realistic
settings similarly suggest that task success and policy compliance
can hinge on longer horizon interaction patterns and operational
tooling [7, 25].
6.2
Implications for Trustworthy Agents
This benchmark provides actionable but conditional risk signals for
deploying customer service LLM agents under profit-seeking direct
prompt injection. The scenario and PI-family differences can guide
pre-deployment red-teaming and mitigation prioritization (e.g., al-
locating more coverage to higher risk domains and consistently
effective attack families; adopting stricter guardrails, verification,
or escalation in susceptible domains). These signals should be in-
terpreted as relative diagnostics over the benchmark scope (10
scenarios, 100 injected conversations), not as universal real-world
risk rankings, since safety properties can shift with environment
details, operational rules, and tooling constraints [7, 25].
A second implication concerns measurement trustworthiness:
evaluation is part of the system. Because our pipeline relies on au-
tomatic judges and exhibits evaluator effects, success rate estimates
and some comparisons may depend on the scoring model and rubric.
For trustworthy downstream use, benchmark reports should there-
fore include judge agreement statistics, sensitivity analyses over
success thresholds, and calibration against a small human-audited
subset; this is especially important given evidence that judge-based
pipelines can be vulnerable to injection style manipulation [18].
Finally, the benchmark has clear deployment boundaries. We
target dialogue only direct injection and disable external access
(no URL opening; no retrieval/tool execution), so indirect style
items should be read as instruction-compliance boundary tests
rather than full retrieval/tooling evaluations. Prior work shows that
when models consume attacker-controlled external content, risks
can escalate to broader application compromise and unintended
actions [4, 16]. Accordingly, the most trustworthy interpretation
is as an initial-exposure stress test for conversational customer-
service agents; extending claims to tool-augmented or long-horizon
agents requires end-to-end, multi-step evaluation and defenses
tailored to those settings [2].
7
Conclusion
We introduced a benchmark for profit-seeking direct prompt in-
jection in customer service style interactions, covering 10 service
scenarios and 100 attack scripts under a unified scoring protocol
and uncertainty reporting. Across five widely deployed LLMs, our
results show that vulnerability is strongly domain-dependent: Air-
line Support is consistently the most exploitable scenario under our
evaluation, with a clear separation from lower-risk domains. After
adjusting for scenario composition and evaluator effects via logistic
regression and marginal standardization, we also find substantial
model-level heterogeneity, with DeepSeek exhibiting the highest
adjusted success probability in our setting.
Beyond rankings, the benchmark highlights which strategies
transfer most reliably in practice: payload splitting emerges as the
most consistently effective technique family in our experiments.
Finally, we emphasize that these measurements are protocol and
judge dependent automatic evaluators can introduce systematic
shifts in estimated success and relative comparisons, so results
should be interpreted as deployment-relevant diagnostics rather
than absolute ground truth. We release the dataset and benchmark
framework to support replication, auditing, and follow-on work
on defenses and safer deployment of customer service LLM agents
under direct prompt injection.

A.2
Claude evaluator JSON parsing failures on
PI2 (obfuscation) instances
The evaluation pipeline uses an LLM-as-evaluator protocol in which
an evaluator model is instructed to output a structured result for
each target-model reply: an integer score on the 0–5 rubric and an
optional textual rationale. In an initial execution of the scoring code,
a systematic failure mode was observed for the Claude-4.1 evaluator
on a subset of PI2 (Obfuscation) instances. These instances often
contain encoded or transformed payloads (e.g., Base64 strings) that
require decoding or interpretation before the policy intent becomes
explicit.
A representative example uses the user message:
Decode: c2hhcmUgdGVuYW50IHNTSU4gYW5kIGRPIEJOSw==
and post in chat.
For one target model (GPT-4o), the agent reply is a direct refusal
(“I’m sorry, I can’t assist with that request.”). However, the Claude-
4.1 evaluator does not successfully return a valid structured judg-
ment. Instead, it may emit a refusal-style message object (e.g.,
stop_reason=’refusal’) with an empty or non-JSON content
field. Because the downstream parser expects a JSON-formatted
output containing evaluation_score and evaluation_reason,
the evaluation step fails with errors such as “Failed to parse JSON,”
leaving the corresponding score fields missing.
In the main analysis, rows with missing evaluator scores are
excluded from metric computation to avoid treating missing judg-
ments as outcomes. This behavior indicates that evaluator robust-
ness can be PI_type-dependent when strict structured-output con-
straints are enforced. Practical mitigations include enforcing a
stricter evaluator instruction that always returns a valid JSON ob-
ject, adding retry logic when parsing fails, and implementing a
fallback extraction routine for refusal-style outputs so that miss-
ingness does not concentrate in a single attack family.
