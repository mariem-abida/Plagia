Abstract—Quantum annealers can solve QUBO problems ef-
ficiently but struggle with continuous optimization tasks like
regression due to their discrete nature. We introduce Quadratic
Continuous Quantum Optimization (QCQO), an anytime algo-
rithm that approximates solutions to unconstrained quadratic
programs via a sequence of QUBO instances. Rather than
encoding real variables as binary vectors, QCQO implicitly
represents them using continuous QUBO weights and iteratively
refines the solution by summing sampled vectors. This allows
flexible control over the number of binary variables and adapts
well to hardware constraints. We prove convergence properties,
introduce a step size adaptation scheme, and validate the method
on linear regression. Experiments with simulated and real quan-
tum annealers show that QCQO achieves accurate results with
fewer qubits, though convergence slows on noisy hardware. Our
approach enables quantum annealing to address a wider class of
continuous problems.
Index Terms—Quantum Annealing, QUBO, Continuous Opti-
mization, Anytime Algorithms, Linear Regression.
I. INTRODUCTION
Quantum Computing (QC) has emerged as a promising
technology for accelerating algorithms, with well-known quan-
tum subroutines such as Grover search [1] or Shor’s algo-
rithm [2] allowing, in theory, for asymptotic speedups using
quantum circuits. However, technical challenges such as quan-
tum gate noise, decoherence and read-out errors prohibit large-
scale application of quantum gate computing at the current
point in time [3].
Quantum Annealing (QA) is an alternative QC paradigm
designed to solve one particular class of optimization prob-
lems [4]. Although it is, unlike quantum circuits, not a
universal computation model, QA has become a valuable
tool in combinatorial optimization, as it can solve various
problems from satisfyability [5] over graph problems [6] to
machine learning [7]. Applications range from finance [8]
over logistics [9], [10] to image processing [11]. The central
optimization problem class that quantum annealers solve is
QUBO.

We typically assume Q to be either symmetrical or an
upper triangle matrix, such that it defines the energy function
uniquely. Every non-symmetrical QUBO instance can be made
symmetrical by applying Q ←(Q + Q⊺)/2. QUBO is gener-
ally strongly NP-hard [12]. Besides QA, a range of solution
or approximation strategies for QUBO exist, both heuristic and
exact [13].
A common approach in literature is to cast optimization
problems arising in applications into QUBO form, solve them
using a quantum annealer and interpreting the resulting min-
imizing binary vector as the solution to the original problem.
While this approach has proven very fruitful for a large number
of problems [6], a strong limitation of QUBO is that it is fun-
damentally combinatorial, having a discrete and finite domain.
This makes it hard to apply it naturally to problems with
a continuous solution space, such as regression or artificial
neural network training, where the variables are real-valued
weights. Nonetheless, QUBO formulations of both of these
problems have been devised, using rough approximations. For
instance, Date et al. [14] uses a precision vector p ∈Rp to
interpret a binary vector z ∈Bnp as digits of a base, such
that the real-valued solution is given by w = P z ∈Rn with
P = 1n⊗p⊺, where 1n denotes the n×n identity matrix, and
⊗is the Kronecker product. Naturally, the elements of w are
still discrete, taking at most 2p distinct values. Moreover, the
number of binary variables is O(np), meaning that doubling
the precision implies doubling the number of variables, which
is a prohibitive factor for contemporary QA hardware.
In this article we present a novel approach to solving
optimization problems with continuous domain on quantum
annealers. To this end, we generalize the idea of precision
vectors by representing numbers as sums of vectors we re-
peatedly sample from a random distribution, refining the result
with every QUBO instance we solve. Our contribution has
a number of advantages: Firstly, we eliminate the necessity
to explicitly represent real numbers as binary vectors by
representing them implicitly through the QUBO weights, which
are already continuous. Secondly, the number of vectors, i.e.,
the number of binary variables to optimize per QUBO instance,

is a hyperparameter that can be chosen freely according to
the hardware capacities, making this approach very flexible.
Lastly, the resulting strategy is an anytime algorithm, as each
consecutive QUBO solution is a refinement of the previous
one. This allows for easy adaption to quantum annealers with
a limited budget.
This paper is structured as follows: In Section II we describe
our proposed continuous optimization procedure. After a dis-
cussion of its theoretical properties in Section III we put it into
practice in Section IV, where we use our method to perform
regression as an exemplary optimization task. We improve the
performance by introducing a step size scheduling scheme in
Section V. Section Section VI discusses the implications of
possible future developments of this method and concludes
this article.

A. Sampling the Matrix R
The choice of distribution R is of central importance for the
behavior of QCQO, as it controls the distribution of possible
update steps R⊺z.
While it is in principle possible to use an arbitrary matrix-
valued distribution, we can sample the rows R1, . . . , Rn
independently from some multivariate distribution, or even
the individual elements Rij from some univariate distribution.
If we sample row-wise and i.i.d. from a multivariate normal
distribution with mean µ and covariance matrix Σ (denoted

reached with a single update step, as follows from the fact
that N(µ, Σ) has full support on Rd. However, we can show
that the distribution of the update step U = R⊺z is closely
related to the distribution of each row.

A. Number of Qubits
Approaches comparable to QCQO use precision vectors to
represent continuous values [7], [14]. For encoding a value
in the unit interval [0, 1] using k (qu)bits, we can construct a
vector p = (2i−1/2k −1)⊺
i=1,...,k so that Xp = {p⊺z : z ∈
Bk} samples the interval evenly in steps of size 2k −1,
with p⊺0 = 0 and p⊺1 = 1. To represent any number
within a neighborhood of size ϵ > 0 we need at least
k ≥⌈log2(1/(2ϵ) + 1)⌉, or Θ(d log(1/ϵ)) (qu)bits.
In contrast, QCQO makes the continuous values implicit
and instead uses (qu)bits to encode the inclusion/exclusion
of fixed summands in a sum. Consequently our method can
encode any arbitrary vector x ∈Rd using just a single bit
zi, by simply choosing Ri = x, i.e., we need Θ(1) binary
variables in general. This allows us to choose the number of
(qu)bits flexibly according to hardware limitations, requiring
fewer qubits than explicit encodings.

A. Experimental Evaluation
To test the convergence behavior of QCQO, we generate a
synthetic regression data set with d = 16 and N = 105 by
(i) sampling w ∈N(0d, Id), (ii) setting w ←100w/∥w∥2,
such that the weight vector has a fixed Euclidean distance
of 100 from the origin, (iii) sampling X ∈RN×d with
Xi ∼N(0d, dId), (iv) setting Xi,d = 1 ∀i, and (v) computing
y = Xw. Following this approach we obtain a regression
data set with ground-truth weight vector w∗, which we try to
recover using QCQO.
To this end, we initialize Alg. 1 with A and a according
to Eq. (6) and choose 0d as our initial guess for every run.
The rows of R are sampled i.i.e. from a normal distribution
N(0d, 4σ/nId), which by Theorem 1 results in update steps
distributed as N(0d, σId) in expectation over uniform z. We
choose all combinations of (n, σ) ∈{8, 16, 24} × {0.1, 1.0}.
Due to the relatively small values of n, we use a brute-force
QUBO solver.
The results are shown in Fig. 1, from which we make two
key observations: Firstly, we observe that convergence is faster
with higher n. This is likely due to the larger number of sums
that can be formed from the rows of R in each iterations.
Increasing n leads to an exponentially larger set of possible
steps in Rd, increasing the probability to find better solutions.
Secondly, a larger σ leads to a steeper initial descent
followed by a shallower descent after an MSE of around 103.
We assume this change in behavior occurs when the distance
between the current solution and the optimal solution becomes
smaller than a standard deviation of U. This is analogous

A. Evaluation on Quantum Annealer
For small n < 30 we were able to compute the ground-
truth optima of the QUBO instances in Algs. 1 and 2 by
brute force, but this quickly becomes infeasible for larger
n ≫30. To investigate the performance of QCQO using
imperfect QUBO solvers, we run it using a D-Wave quantum
annealer. Specifically, we use the Advantage 2 system 1.1,
which comprises over 5,000 qubits and up to around 35,000
couplings using the Pegasus topology [19], allowing for dense
QUBO weight matrices of up to n = 180. We solve each
QUBO instance with an annealing time of 20 ns and 100
reads (“shots”): the annealing process is repeated 100 times
per instance and the lowest-energy solution returned. Due to
persisting challenges of real quantum hardware, this solution
may not be optimal [20]. However, QCQO should still work,
as long as the solution returned by the solver does not increase
the loss value. This is highly unlikely, as 0n is always a valid
solution leaving wt unchanged.
We repeat the same experiment described in Section V,
but replace the brute-force QUBO solver with calls to the
D-Wave device. Additionally, we run the algorithm with
n ∈{16, 32, 64}, as the QA device allows us to solve larger
QUBO instances. The resulting MSE over time is shown in
Fig. 5: We clearly observe a very similar behavior as in Fig. 3,
although the decrease in MSE is less steep, leading to a final
MSE of only around 10−1 after 1000 iterations for n = 64.

VI. CONCLUSION
In this work we have described the Quadratic Continu-
ous Quantum Optimization (QCQO) algorithm, approximating
solutions of unconstrained quadratic programming problems
using a series of QUBO problems, which can be solved by
QA. We demonstrated that the algorithm works and finds good
solutions to an LR problem. In addition, we devised a step
size adaption scheme that demonstrably improves convergence
speed and solution quality. Lastly, we showed that QCQO
also works on real quantum hardware by running it on a D-
Wave Advantage 2, though the reduced solution quality leads
to slower convergence.
In the scope of this work, we only used LR as an exem-
plary optimization problem, which has a closed-form solution,
however, the class of problems that can be approached by
QCQO is much larger and comprises harder problems. In
particular, it can be applied naturally to all unconstrained
quadratic programming problems with indefinite parameter
matrix A, which arise in diverse settings, including relaxations
of combinatorial problems such as Max-Cut and QUBO, as
subproblems in robust and nonconvex optimization [21], or
as intermediate formulations in finance and signal processing;
our method thus offers potential utility as a heuristic in these
domains. In future work, we will explore the efficiency of our
approach on other problems, particularly such with non-convex
loss functions.
So far, the matrix R is always sampled from some distribu-
tion. While this approach has desirable theoretical properties,
the question arises if some fixed choice of R leads to the
best possible coverage of the hyperball around wt across all
z, or if we can adapt R (or its distribution parameters) based
on the previous update steps, similarly to covariance matrix
adaption [22].
Our step size adaption scheme (Section V), while effective
in our experiments, is largely heuristic. An in-depth analysis
of different schemes and how they impact the convergence
properties and efficiency of QCQO is left for future work.
